Bifurcations

A bifurcation occurs when changing a parameter causes a qualitative change in the behaviour of a dynamical system. The parameter may change gradually, but the structure of the long-term dynamics can change fundamentally at a critical value.

Core idea. A parameter controls the system. At most parameter values, a small parameter change produces only a small change in behaviour. At a bifurcation value, the type or number of long-term states can change.

Parameters versus state variables

Consider

\[\frac{dx}{dt}=f(x;\mu).\]

The state variable \(x(t)\) changes with time. The quantity \(\mu\) is a parameter: during one model run it is usually treated as fixed.

Bifurcation analysis asks what happens to the system when we repeat the analysis for different values of \(\mu\).

A simple way to think about it

Suppose \(\mu\) represents transmission strength, harvesting pressure, mortality, nutrient supply or an interaction coefficient.

We gradually vary \(\mu\), find the equilibria for each value, determine their stability, and look for critical parameter values where the qualitative picture changes.

What counts as a qualitative change?

Examples include an equilibrium appearing or disappearing, two equilibria colliding, equilibria exchanging stability, a stable state becoming unstable, or a stable oscillation emerging.

A mere numerical shift in the location of an otherwise unchanged stable equilibrium is not by itself a bifurcation.

Bifurcation diagrams

A bifurcation diagram usually places the parameter on the horizontal axis and an equilibrium value on the vertical axis.

Each point on an equilibrium branch answers: if the parameter has this value, where is an equilibrium?

Solid branches conventionally represent stable equilibria and dashed branches unstable equilibria.

A bifurcation diagram is not a time-series graph. Its horizontal axis is a parameter, not time.

1. Saddle-node bifurcation

Consider the normal form

\[\frac{dx}{dt}=\mu-x^2.\]

Equilibria satisfy

\[\mu-x^2=0,\]

so

\[x^*=\pm\sqrt{\mu}.\]

For \(\mu\lt0\), there are no real equilibria. At \(\mu=0\), the two branches meet. For \(\mu\gt0\), two equilibria exist.

Which saddle-node branch is stable?

Here

\[f'(x)=-2x.\]

At \(x^*=+\sqrt{\mu}\), the derivative is negative, so the upper branch is stable. At \(x^*=-\sqrt{\mu}\), it is positive, so the lower branch is unstable.

Saddle-node bifurcation. A stable and an unstable equilibrium meet at the critical parameter value and disappear on the other side.

Biological meaning of a saddle-node

Saddle-node bifurcations are important when a biological system has alternative states separated by an unstable threshold. As a parameter changes, a stable state and the threshold can collide and disappear.

The system can then be forced toward a very different state. This is one mathematical mechanism behind abrupt regime shifts.

2. Transcritical bifurcation

Consider

\[\frac{dx}{dt}=\mu x-x^2=x(\mu-x).\]

The equilibria are

\[x^*=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad x^*=\mu.\]

The two branches exist on both sides of \(\mu=0\), cross there, and exchange stability.

Check the stability

Since

\[f'(x)=\mu-2x,\]

at \(x^*=0\),

\[f'(0)=\mu,\]

so the zero equilibrium is stable for \(\mu\lt0\) and unstable for \(\mu\gt0\).

At \(x^*=\mu\),

\[f'(\mu)=-\mu,\]

so the nonzero branch has the opposite stability.

Transcritical bifurcation. Two equilibrium branches cross and exchange stability at the critical value.

Why transcritical bifurcations matter in epidemiology

Disease-free and endemic equilibria commonly exchange stability at an invasion threshold. In many epidemic models this threshold is expressed using the basic reproduction number \(R_0\).

Typically, the disease-free equilibrium is stable when

\[R_0\lt1,\]

and loses stability as \(R_0\) passes through 1. A biologically feasible endemic equilibrium can then become stable.

The threshold \(R_0=1\) is not important merely because of the number 1. It is important because the local growth behaviour of infection changes sign there.

3. Pitchfork bifurcation

A standard supercritical pitchfork is

\[\frac{dx}{dt}=\mu x-x^3=x(\mu-x^2).\]

The equilibria satisfy

\[x^*=0\]

for every \(\mu\), and for \(\mu\ge0\),

\[x^*=\pm\sqrt{\mu}.\]

As \(\mu\) crosses zero, the central equilibrium loses stability and two stable branches emerge.

Supercritical pitchfork. One stable state loses stability while two new stable states emerge symmetrically.

When pitchforks arise

Pitchfork bifurcations are closely associated with symmetry. They are useful idealised models for spontaneous symmetry breaking, although exact pitchfork structure is less generic when biological asymmetries are present.

4. Hopf bifurcation

A Hopf bifurcation occurs in a system of at least two dimensions when a complex-conjugate pair of eigenvalues crosses the imaginary axis.

Write the pair as

\[\lambda_{1,2}(\mu)=a(\mu)\pm ib(\mu).\]

The critical change occurs when

\[a(\mu_c)=0\]

and the real part changes sign as \(\mu\) passes through \(\mu_c\).

Why a Hopf bifurcation creates oscillatory behaviour

The imaginary part produces rotation. Before the bifurcation, a negative real part may make oscillations decay toward equilibrium. After the real part changes sign, that equilibrium can become unstable and a periodic orbit may appear.

In a supercritical Hopf bifurcation, a small stable limit cycle typically emerges as the equilibrium loses stability.

Schematic supercritical Hopf bifurcation. Before the threshold, disturbances decay to equilibrium; beyond it, a stable oscillation can surround the now-unstable equilibrium.

Biological meaning of Hopf bifurcations

A Hopf bifurcation can mark the onset of sustained oscillations in predator–prey systems, host–parasite models, biochemical networks, epidemiological models or physiological feedback systems.

The key change is not simply that variables fluctuate, but that the long-term attractor changes from an equilibrium to a periodic orbit.

Connection with eigenvalues

Bifurcations are often detected by watching eigenvalues of the Jacobian as a parameter changes.

For common equilibrium bifurcations such as saddle-node, transcritical and pitchfork, a real eigenvalue passes through zero. For a Hopf bifurcation, a complex pair crosses the imaginary axis.

Stability changes when an eigenvalue reaches the boundary between decay and growth.

Critical parameter value

The parameter value \(\mu_c\) at which the qualitative change occurs is called a bifurcation value or critical value.

Close to this value, the system may become unusually sensitive and recovery from disturbances can become slow because a relevant eigenvalue approaches zero real part.

Critical slowing down

Suppose the dominant stable eigenvalue approaches zero from below:

\[\lambda(\mu)\to0^-.\]

Then the decay factor

\[e^{\lambda t}\]

decays increasingly slowly. The system takes longer to return after a small perturbation.

This phenomenon is called critical slowing down and can sometimes provide information about proximity to certain transitions.

Critical slowing down is not a universal warning signal. Its usefulness depends on the type of transition, the model, noise and the available observations.

Bifurcation versus threshold

Not every threshold is mathematically a bifurcation. A threshold becomes a bifurcation when crossing it changes the qualitative structure of the dynamical system.

Likewise, a parameter value that is operationally important in biology may be a useful decision threshold without being a bifurcation.

Bifurcation versus stochastic switching

In a deterministic model, a bifurcation results from changing a parameter. In a stochastic system, random fluctuations can sometimes move the system between states even while parameters remain fixed.

These are different mechanisms, although they can interact in real biological systems.

Local and global bifurcations

The examples above are mainly local bifurcations, which can be detected from behaviour near an equilibrium.

Global bifurcations involve larger structures in state space, such as the creation or destruction of certain connecting or periodic trajectories. They generally require more than local Jacobian analysis.

Why bifurcation diagrams are useful for decisions

A bifurcation diagram shows how possible long-term outcomes depend on a controllable or uncertain parameter. It can reveal ranges with one stable outcome, ranges with multiple outcomes, and critical values where stability changes.

In biological applications this can help identify parameter regions associated with persistence, extinction, invasion, coexistence or oscillation.

A practical workflow

Choose a biologically meaningful parameter. Solve the equilibrium equations as functions of that parameter. Determine equilibrium stability, usually from the Jacobian eigenvalues. Plot equilibrium branches against the parameter and distinguish stable from unstable branches. Locate values where branches meet or stability changes. Then interpret those critical values in terms of the biological model.

Key idea. Bifurcation analysis studies how the qualitative dynamics change as parameters change. It connects equilibria, stability and eigenvalues to biologically important transitions such as invasion, collapse, alternative states and the onset of oscillations.
