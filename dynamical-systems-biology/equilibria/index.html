Equilibria

An equilibrium is a state at which the model has no instantaneous tendency to change. If the system starts exactly there and the parameters remain fixed, it stays there.

Core idea. An equilibrium answers the question: “At which state do all gains and losses balance exactly?” It does not yet tell us whether that state is stable.

One-dimensional systems

For

\[\frac{dx}{dt}=f(x),\]

an equilibrium \(x^*\) satisfies

\[\boxed{f(x^*)=0}.\]

This means the instantaneous rate of change is zero at \(x=x^*\).

Why setting the derivative to zero works

The derivative tells us the direction and speed of change. If

\[\frac{dx}{dt}\gt0,\]

the state increases. If

\[\frac{dx}{dt}\lt0,\]

the state decreases. At equilibrium, neither happens, so

\[\frac{dx}{dt}=0.\]

A simple example

Consider

\[\frac{dx}{dt}=3-x.\]

Set the right-hand side equal to zero:

\[3-x=0.\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{x^*=3}.\]

If \(x=3\), then \(dx/dt=0\), so the state remains constant.

Equilibria are states, not necessarily trajectories

The equilibrium value \(x^*=3\) is one special state. A trajectory starting at \(x=1\) is not an equilibrium, even if it later approaches 3.

Important distinction. An equilibrium is a fixed state. A solution trajectory is the path the system follows through time.

Phase-line intuition

For \(dx/dt=3-x\), states below 3 move upward and states above 3 move downward. The equilibrium is where the arrows meet.

Biological example: logistic growth

For

\[\frac{dN}{dt}=rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right),\]

equilibria satisfy

\[rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right)=0.\]

So

\[\boxed{N^*=0\qquad\text{or}\qquad N^*=K}.\]

The first equilibrium represents extinction. The second represents the carrying-capacity state of this model.

How do we find all equilibria?

Factorisation is often useful. In logistic growth, the product is zero when either factor is zero:

\[N=0\]

or

\[1-\frac{N}{K}=0.\]

This gives the two equilibria above.

Biological feasibility matters

Mathematical equations can produce equilibrium values that are impossible biologically.

For example, a negative population equilibrium may be mathematically valid algebraically but biologically meaningless.

Always check feasibility. In population models, equilibrium populations usually need to be non-negative. Other models may have additional biological constraints.

Equilibria in systems of equations

For a system

\[\frac{d\mathbf x}{dt}=\mathbf F(\mathbf x),\]

an equilibrium \(\mathbf x^*\) satisfies

\[\boxed{\mathbf F(\mathbf x^*)=\mathbf 0}.\]

Every component derivative must be zero at the same time.

Two-species example

Suppose

\[\frac{dx}{dt}=x(1-x-y),\] \[\frac{dy}{dt}=y(1-y).\]

At equilibrium, both equations must vanish simultaneously.

The second equation gives

\[y=0\qquad\text{or}\qquad y=1.\]

Each case is then substituted into the first equation to find compatible \(x\)-values.

Nullclines help in two-dimensional systems

A nullcline is a set of states where one derivative is zero.

For a system

\[\frac{dx}{dt}=f(x,y),\qquad\frac{dy}{dt}=g(x,y),\]

the \(x\)-nullcline is defined by

\[f(x,y)=0,\]

and the \(y\)-nullcline by

\[g(x,y)=0.\]

Equilibria occur at intersections of the nullclines because both derivatives are zero there.

Nullcline intersection

In a two-dimensional system, an equilibrium occurs where the two nullclines intersect. At that point both state variables are instantaneously stationary.

Boundary and interior equilibria

In biological systems it is useful to distinguish different types of equilibrium.

TypeMeaning
boundary equilibriumone or more populations are zero
interior equilibriumall populations are positive
disease-free equilibriuminfected compartments are zero
endemic equilibriuminfection remains present at a positive level
coexistence equilibriummultiple species remain positive

Example from an epidemic model

In an SIS model, a disease-free equilibrium has

\[I^*=0.\]

An endemic equilibrium has

\[I^*\gt0.\]

Whether the endemic equilibrium exists and is biologically feasible depends on the model parameters.

Existence can depend on parameters

Some equilibria exist only when parameters satisfy certain conditions.

For example, an endemic equilibrium may require a transmission parameter to exceed a threshold. Below that threshold, the algebraic expression may become non-positive and therefore biologically infeasible.

Parameter thresholds often appear because equilibrium formulas must satisfy biological constraints.

Equilibrium does not mean no activity

A biological equilibrium can contain ongoing events. Births and deaths may both occur, infections and recoveries may continue, or individuals may move between compartments.

What is zero is the net rate of change, not necessarily every underlying process.

Example. At an endemic equilibrium, new infections may occur continuously, but they are balanced by recovery and other losses so the compartment sizes remain constant.

Equilibrium versus steady state

In autonomous ordinary differential equations, the terms equilibrium and steady state are often used almost interchangeably.

The essential idea is that the state variables remain constant in time.

Equilibrium versus long-term behaviour

A system does not have to approach an equilibrium. It may oscillate, approach a limit cycle, behave chaotically, or leave the biologically relevant region.

Finding equilibria is therefore an important first step, not a complete description of the dynamics.

Equilibrium versus stability

Once an equilibrium has been found, the next question is what happens after a small disturbance.

If nearby trajectories return, the equilibrium is stable. If they move away, it is unstable.

Finding an equilibrium and determining its stability are separate calculations.

Why equilibria matter in biology

Equilibria can represent persistent population levels, extinction states, coexistence of species, disease-free states, endemic infection, chemical concentrations, tumour states or other long-term configurations.

They provide reference states around which stability, bifurcations, intervention thresholds and stochastic fluctuations can be analysed.

A practical workflow

For a biological ODE model, a useful sequence is to set every derivative equal to zero, solve the resulting algebraic equations, list all mathematical solutions, remove biologically infeasible solutions, interpret the remaining equilibria, and then analyse their stability.

Key idea. An equilibrium is a state where all net rates of change are zero. In one dimension it solves \(f(x^*)=0\); in several dimensions it solves all component equations simultaneously. Biological interpretation and feasibility must be checked before stability is analysed.
