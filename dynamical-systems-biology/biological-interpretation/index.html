Biological interpretation

Dynamical-systems mathematics tells us how model variables change. Biological interpretation asks what those mathematical results mean for the organisms, populations, infections or physiological processes represented by the model.

Core idea. Do not stop at “the equilibrium is stable” or “an eigenvalue is positive.” Translate the mathematical statement into a biological statement about persistence, invasion, recovery, extinction, coexistence or oscillation.

Three layers of a mathematical biology model

It is useful to keep three levels separate:

LevelQuestionExample
biologyWhat process are we trying to represent?infection spreads through contact
mathematical modelHow is that process represented?an infection term such as \(\beta SI/N\)
dynamical analysisWhat behaviour follows from the equations?infection grows or decays near the disease-free state

Interpretation reconnects the third level to the first.

State variables must have a biological meaning

Before interpreting a graph or equilibrium, identify what each variable measures. For example, \(N(t)\) may be population abundance, \(I(t)\) infectious individuals, and \(P(t)\) predator abundance.

The same mathematical value has different meanings in different models. The statement \(x=0\) might mean extinction, absence of infection, zero concentration, or absence of a phenotype.

Parameters also carry meaning

A parameter should be interpreted according to how it enters the model. For example, \(r\) may describe intrinsic population growth, \(K\) carrying capacity, \(\beta\) transmission strength and \(\gamma\) recovery rate.

A parameter is not merely a number fitted to data. Its units and biological role determine how changes in it should be interpreted.

Equilibria are candidate long-term states

An equilibrium \(\mathbf{x}^*\) satisfies

\[\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=\mathbf{0}.\]

Mathematically, the state does not change there. Biologically, an equilibrium can represent a persistent balance of processes.

Examples include extinction, disease-free population, endemic infection, single-species persistence, coexistence of interacting species, or a regulated physiological concentration.

An equilibrium is not necessarily biologically possible

Solving equilibrium equations can produce negative population sizes or concentrations. Such solutions may be mathematically valid roots but biologically infeasible.

Always check feasibility. In population models, negative abundances are normally outside the biologically meaningful state space.

Zero equilibrium: what does it mean?

For

\[\frac{dN}{dt}=rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right),\]

one equilibrium is

\[N^*=0.\]

Here it represents population extinction. In an epidemic model, however, \(I^*=0\) may represent absence of infection rather than extinction of the host population.

The mathematics alone does not tell us which interpretation is correct; the definition of the variable does.

Positive equilibrium: persistence or coexistence

A positive equilibrium in a one-population model can represent persistence at a constant abundance. In a two-species system, an equilibrium with

\[x^*>0,\qquad y^*>0\]

can represent coexistence.

But existence alone is not enough. We must also ask whether the equilibrium is stable and whether it is reachable from biologically relevant initial conditions.

Stability as response to disturbance

Suppose a population is at an equilibrium and is disturbed slightly. A locally stable equilibrium causes sufficiently small disturbances to decay.

Biologically, this can be interpreted as a form of local resilience: the model tends to restore the state after a small disturbance.

Near a stable state, small disturbances are restored. Near an unstable state, small disturbances are amplified.

Stable does not mean “good”

Stability is a mathematical property, not a value judgement. An undesirable biological state can be stable.

For example, an endemic disease state, a degraded ecological state, or a tumour-dominated state could be stable within a model.

Stable means disturbances tend to decay locally. It does not mean healthy, desirable or optimal.

Unstable does not mean impossible

An unstable equilibrium is still an equilibrium. If the model begins exactly there, it remains there in an ideal deterministic system.

But an arbitrarily small perturbation in an unstable direction can move the system away, so the state is not robust to disturbance.

Local versus global biological resilience

Local stability concerns sufficiently small disturbances. A large disturbance may push the system far from the equilibrium, possibly into another basin of attraction.

Therefore local asymptotic stability should not be translated as “the biological system always returns after any disturbance.”

Basins of attraction

If a model has more than one stable long-term state, the initial condition can determine which one is reached. The set of initial states that approach a particular attractor is its basin of attraction.

An unstable threshold can separate the basins of two stable states. A sufficiently large disturbance can move the system from one basin to another.

Eigenvalues become biological rates of local response

After linearisation near an equilibrium, eigenvalues describe how small disturbance modes change.

If

\[\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)<0,\]

that mode decays. If

\[\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)>0,\]

it grows.

Biologically, negative eigenvalues can represent recovery toward a state, while a positive eigenvalue can represent successful invasion or amplification of a disturbance.

The size of an eigenvalue matters

An eigenvalue close to zero on the negative side corresponds to slow local recovery. A more negative eigenvalue corresponds to faster decay of that mode.

Thus eigenvalues can contain information about both stability and the timescale of recovery.

Complex eigenvalues mean oscillatory response

If

\[\lambda=a\pm ib,\]

the real part \(a\) controls growth or decay and the imaginary part \(b\) produces oscillation.

A negative real part therefore corresponds to damped biological oscillations around equilibrium. A positive real part corresponds to growing oscillatory departure.

Nullclines have biological interpretations

For a two-variable model, an \(x\)-nullcline is where

\[\frac{dx}{dt}=0.\]

This does not necessarily mean the whole system is stationary. It means only that \(x\) is instantaneously neither increasing nor decreasing there.

Similarly, on a \(y\)-nullcline, \(y\) is momentarily unchanged.

Their intersections are equilibria because both rates are zero simultaneously.

Phase planes tell us possible joint behaviour

A time series tells us how variables change with time. A phase plane instead shows how variables move relative to one another.

This can reveal attraction, repulsion, thresholds, coexistence states, oscillatory motion and alternative outcomes that may be harder to see from separate time-series plots.

Limit cycles mean persistent rhythms

A stable limit cycle represents a repeating biological state that attracts nearby trajectories. The system does not settle to constant values; it settles to a repeating pattern.

This can model self-sustained ecological, biochemical or physiological rhythms.

But repeated waves in observed data do not automatically prove the existence of a deterministic limit cycle. Seasonal forcing and stochastic fluctuations can also generate repeated patterns.

Bifurcations represent qualitative transitions

A bifurcation occurs when changing a parameter changes the qualitative dynamics.

Biologically, this can correspond to invasion, loss of persistence, onset of coexistence, disappearance of a stable state, or emergence of sustained oscillation.

An epidemic threshold example

At a disease-free equilibrium, consider introducing a very small number of infectious individuals. If the infected disturbance decays, the disease-free state is locally stable to invasion. If it grows, the disease can invade under the model assumptions.

In many epidemic models this change occurs as the basic reproduction number passes through

\[R_0=1.\]

The biological interpretation is therefore about invasion potential: can a small introduction grow?

A population threshold example

In a model with a strong Allee effect, a positive unstable equilibrium can separate extinction from persistence.

Below the threshold, population growth is insufficient and abundance moves toward zero. Above it, the population may move toward a positive stable equilibrium.

The unstable equilibrium therefore has a direct biological interpretation as a critical population threshold within the model.

Predator–prey interpretation

In a predator–prey phase plane, arrows describe simultaneous changes in prey and predator abundance. A closed or spiralling trajectory represents coupled changes rather than two unrelated oscillations.

A lag between prey and predator peaks can arise because predator growth responds to prey abundance after prey have increased.

Competition interpretation

Competition models can produce exclusion, coexistence or alternative stable outcomes depending on parameter values.

A mathematically stable coexistence equilibrium means small changes in both species tend to decay. It does not prove that coexistence occurs from every possible initial population size.

Parameter changes versus state disturbances

These are conceptually different.

A state disturbance changes the current values of variables, such as suddenly reducing a population. A parameter change modifies the rules of the dynamics, such as changing mortality or transmission.

Stability primarily asks about state disturbances with parameters fixed. Bifurcation analysis asks what happens when parameters themselves change.

Transient behaviour matters

Two models can approach the same equilibrium but have very different transient paths. One may approach monotonically, another with large overshoots, and another with slowly damped oscillations.

These differences can be biologically important when temporary peaks, troughs or delays have practical consequences.

Mathematical possibility versus biological plausibility

A mathematical model can generate behaviour that is internally consistent but biologically implausible because of unrealistic assumptions or parameter values.

Interpretation therefore requires checking units, parameter ranges, feasible states and whether the model mechanisms match the biological question.

Model assumptions limit the conclusion

If a model assumes homogeneous mixing, constant parameters or no spatial structure, conclusions apply within those assumptions.

The mathematics may be correct while the biological prediction is inaccurate because an important mechanism was omitted.

Mathematical certainty inside a model is not the same as certainty about the real biological system.

Deterministic versus stochastic interpretation

A deterministic trajectory gives one path for fixed initial conditions and parameters. A stochastic model instead describes a distribution of possible paths.

Thus a deterministic stable state does not eliminate the possibility of stochastic extinction, random outbreaks or noise-driven transitions in a corresponding stochastic model.

A good interpretation workflow

First state what every variable and parameter represents. Check that the mathematical state is biologically feasible. Describe the mathematical result precisely. Translate it into biological language. State whether the conclusion is local or global. Identify the assumptions on which the interpretation depends. Finally, distinguish what the model demonstrates from what would require empirical validation.

Weak interpretation: “The eigenvalues are negative, therefore the system is stable.”

Better interpretation: “Both eigenvalues have negative real parts, so sufficiently small disturbances from the coexistence equilibrium decay in the model. The coexistence state is therefore locally asymptotically stable under the assumed parameter values.”
Key principle. Dynamical-systems analysis becomes mathematical biology only when equilibria, stability, eigenvalues, phase-plane structure, oscillations and bifurcations are translated into statements about the biological processes represented by the model, with the model assumptions kept explicit.
