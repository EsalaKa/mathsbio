Harvesting

Harvesting models add deliberate removal to population growth. Depending on the biological setting, harvesting can represent fishing, hunting, culling, forestry, collection of organisms or another process that removes individuals from a population.

Core idea. A population can persist under harvesting only when its biological production can compensate for removal. The mathematical form of the harvest matters: removing a fixed number per unit time behaves very differently from removing a fixed proportion of the population.

Begin with the unharvested population

For logistic growth,

\[\frac{dN}{dt}=rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right).\]

The natural production rate is therefore

\[G(N)=rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right).\]

It is zero at \(N=0\) and \(N=K\), and reaches its maximum at \(N=K/2\):

\[\boxed{G_{\max}=\frac{rK}{4}}.\]

Harvesting subtracts from this natural population change.

Constant harvesting

Suppose the same number of individuals is removed per unit time, regardless of the current population size. Let \(H\) denote that removal rate. Then

\[\boxed{\frac{dN}{dt}=rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right)-H}.\]

See the balance graphically

Natural logistic production is the curved line. A constant harvest is horizontal because the same number is removed regardless of population size. Intersections are equilibria. The maximum possible natural production is \(rK/4\) at \(N=K/2\).

Equilibria under constant harvesting

At equilibrium, natural production exactly balances harvesting:

\[rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right)=H.\]

Rearranging gives a quadratic equation. Its solutions are

\[\boxed{N_{\pm}=\frac{K}{2}\left(1\pm\sqrt{1-\frac{4H}{rK}}\right)}.\]

Real positive equilibria exist only when

\[\boxed{H\leq\frac{rK}{4}}.\]

Why the lower equilibrium is dangerous

When \(0

If the population is between \(N_-\) and \(N_+\), natural production exceeds the harvest and the population increases. Above \(N_+\), density limitation makes natural production smaller than the harvest, so the population decreases toward \(N_+\).

But below \(N_-\), natural production is again smaller than the fixed harvest, so the population declines further.

Thus constant harvesting creates a threshold. The upper equilibrium \(N_+\) is stable, while the lower equilibrium \(N_-\) is unstable. Falling below the lower threshold produces continued decline in the mathematical model.

Why can a fixed harvest become dangerous at low abundance?

Suppose 20 individuals are removed every year. When the population contains thousands of individuals, that removal may be small relative to biological production. If the population falls to only a few dozen individuals, removing 20 each year becomes enormous relative to the remaining population.

Constant harvesting does not automatically weaken when the population becomes scarce.

The maximum sustainable yield idea

In the simple deterministic logistic model, natural production is greatest at

\[N=\frac{K}{2},\]

where

\[G(N)=\frac{rK}{4}.\]

This motivates the theoretical maximum sustainable yield

\[\boxed{\text{MSY}=\frac{rK}{4}}.\]
This is a mathematical benchmark, not automatically a safe management target. It assumes that \(r\), \(K\), population size and the logistic model are known accurately and remain stable. Real populations experience environmental variability, measurement error, age structure and stochasticity.

Why harvesting exactly at the theoretical maximum is fragile

At \(H=rK/4\), the equilibrium occurs at \(N=K/2\). The harvest line just touches the production curve at its maximum.

There is no margin for a downward error in population size or an upward error in the harvest. If abundance falls below \(K/2\), natural production becomes smaller than the fixed harvest.

This is why practical management generally needs safety margins rather than treating the deterministic maximum as guaranteed sustainable removal.

A numerical example

Suppose

\[r=0.4\text{ per year},\qquad K=1000.\]

The maximum natural production is

\[\frac{rK}{4}=\frac{0.4(1000)}{4}=100\text{ individuals per year}.\]

If \(H=60\), then

\[N_{\pm}=500\left(1\pm\sqrt{1-0.6}\right),\]

giving approximately

\[N_-\approx184,\qquad N_+\approx816.\]

A population near 816 can balance the harvest, but a population pushed below about 184 enters the declining region of this deterministic model.

Population trajectories under a constant harvest

For a harvest below the theoretical maximum, starting above the lower threshold leads toward the stable upper equilibrium. Starting below the threshold leads downward. The mathematical equation can continue to negative values, but a biological population should instead be regarded as depleted or extinct when it reaches zero.

A modelling issue at zero

The expression \(-H\) continues removing individuals even when \(N\) is extremely small. Mathematically, the differential equation can therefore pass below zero, which is biologically impossible.

In applications, harvesting must stop when no individuals remain, or the model must be modified near zero. This illustrates why equations must always be interpreted biologically rather than used mechanically.

Proportional harvesting

A different assumption is that a fixed proportion of the population is removed per unit time. If \(h\) is the per-capita harvesting rate,

\[\boxed{\frac{dN}{dt}=rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right)-hN}.\]

Factorising gives

\[\frac{dN}{dt}=N\left[r\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right)-h\right].\]

Now the amount harvested is \(hN\), so removal automatically becomes smaller when the population becomes smaller.

Equilibrium under proportional harvesting

For a positive equilibrium,

\[r\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right)-h=0.\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{N^*=K\left(1-\frac{h}{r}\right)}.\]

Harvesting effort and catch

In fisheries and other resource models, harvesting is often represented as

\[H(N)=qEN,\]

where \(E\) is harvesting effort and \(q\) is catchability. This is a proportional-harvest model with

\[h=qE.\]

It connects management effort to the biological removal rate.

Connection with Allee effects

Constant harvesting can itself create threshold-like dynamics even when the unharvested population follows ordinary logistic growth. If the population also has a biological Allee effect, harvesting and low-density biological disadvantage can act together and make depletion more serious.

Deterministic sustainability versus real risk

In a deterministic model, a harvest may be labelled sustainable because a positive stable equilibrium exists. In reality, environmental variation and random births and deaths can push abundance away from that equilibrium.

For management questions, it can therefore be more useful to ask for the probability of falling below a critical population size over a specified time period rather than relying only on a deterministic equilibrium.

Key idea. Harvesting is a balance between biological production and removal. Constant harvesting can create an unstable lower threshold and has a theoretical maximum \(rK/4\) under logistic growth. Proportional harvesting weakens automatically as abundance falls. Which formulation is appropriate depends on how harvesting actually operates, and real management requires uncertainty and biological constraints to be considered as well.
