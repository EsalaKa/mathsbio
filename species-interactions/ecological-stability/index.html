Ecological stability

Ecological stability describes how a biological system responds to disturbance. The word stability is used in several different ways, so a model must state exactly which meaning is being studied.

Core idea. A stable system does not necessarily mean “nothing changes.” It means disturbances are controlled in some specified sense: they may decay, remain bounded, or fail to push the system into a different long-term state.

Start with an equilibrium

Consider a system of interacting populations

\[\frac{d\mathbf N}{dt}=\mathbf F(\mathbf N),\]

where

\[\mathbf N=(N_1,N_2,\ldots,N_m)^T.\]

An equilibrium \(\mathbf N^*\) satisfies

\[\mathbf F(\mathbf N^*)=\mathbf 0.\]

At equilibrium, every population has zero instantaneous rate of change.

What does local stability mean?

Suppose the system is at \(\mathbf N^*\), then receives a small disturbance. If the resulting trajectory moves back toward \(\mathbf N^*\), the equilibrium is locally stable.

If nearby trajectories move away instead, the equilibrium is unstable.

Example. A population at carrying capacity can be locally stable because a small increase above equilibrium produces negative growth, while a small decrease produces positive growth.

Stable and unstable equilibria visually

Left: arrows point toward the equilibrium, so small disturbances decay. Right: arrows point away from the equilibrium, so small disturbances grow.

Why linearisation is useful

Near an equilibrium, a nonlinear system can often be approximated by a linear system. Write a small displacement as

\[\mathbf x=\mathbf N-\mathbf N^*.\]

Then, near the equilibrium,

\[\frac{d\mathbf x}{dt}\approx J\mathbf x,\]

where \(J\) is the Jacobian matrix evaluated at the equilibrium.

The Jacobian matrix

\[\boxed{J=\left[\frac{\partial F_i}{\partial N_j}\right]_{\mathbf N=\mathbf N^*}}.\]

The entry \(J_{ij}\) measures how a small change in species \(j\) changes the instantaneous growth rate of species \(i\), close to the equilibrium.

Biological interpretation. The Jacobian is a local interaction matrix. Its entries describe the strengths and signs of direct effects near a particular ecological state.

Why eigenvalues determine local stability

The linearised system

\[\frac{d\mathbf x}{dt}=J\mathbf x\]

can be decomposed into directions associated with the eigenvalues of \(J\). Along each eigen-direction, the disturbance behaves approximately like an exponential function.

If an eigenvalue has real part \(\lambda_r\), its contribution behaves like

\[e^{\lambda_r t}.\]

If \(\lambda_r\lt0\), that component decays. If \(\lambda_r\gt0\), it grows.

The local stability criterion

An equilibrium is locally asymptotically stable when

\[\boxed{\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i)\lt0\quad\text{for every eigenvalue }\lambda_i\text{ of }J}.\]

If at least one eigenvalue has positive real part, the equilibrium is unstable.

What if eigenvalues are complex?

A complex eigenvalue has the form

\[\lambda=a+bi.\]

The real part \(a\) controls whether the disturbance grows or decays. The imaginary part \(b\) contributes oscillatory behaviour.

Eigenvalue patternLocal behaviour
negative real eigenvaluesreturn without oscillation
complex eigenvalues with negative real partdamped oscillations toward equilibrium
complex eigenvalues with positive real partoscillations that grow away from equilibrium
positive real eigenvaluedisturbance grows in at least one direction

Damped oscillations

A stable system can oscillate after a disturbance. Stability concerns the long-term decay of the disturbance, not whether the return is monotonic.

Local stability is not global stability

Local stability describes sufficiently small disturbances. A large disturbance may carry the system far outside the region where the local approximation is valid.

A system can therefore be locally stable near one equilibrium while still having another attractor elsewhere.

Basins of attraction

The basin of attraction of an equilibrium is the set of initial states whose trajectories eventually approach that equilibrium.

If two stable equilibria exist, the state space can be divided into different basins. Crossing a boundary between them can lead to a completely different long-term outcome.

Alternative stable states

Some ecological systems can have more than one stable state under the same external conditions. For example, a lake model might allow both a clear-water state and a turbid state.

A sufficiently large disturbance can move the system from one basin of attraction to another.

Return to equilibrium is not guaranteed after every disturbance. If alternative stable states exist, a large disturbance can trigger a regime shift.

Resistance

Resistance describes how little a system changes when disturbed.

A highly resistant system may show only a small displacement after a disturbance.

Resilience

Resilience often refers to how rapidly a system returns after disturbance, although the term is used differently across ecological literature.

Near a stable equilibrium, the dominant eigenvalue can provide a local recovery-rate measure. If the eigenvalue with real part closest to zero is \(\lambda_d\), recovery is often slower when

\[\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_d)\]

is close to zero from below.

Resistance and resilience are different

PropertyQuestion
resistanceHow far does the system move when disturbed?
resilience / recovery rateHow quickly does it return?

A system can be highly resistant but slow to recover, or strongly displaced but quick to recover.

Persistence

Another meaning of stability is persistence: whether species remain present through time rather than going extinct.

An equilibrium can be mathematically stable while excluding one species, so equilibrium stability and community persistence are not the same concept.

Variability

A system with populations that fluctuate strongly through time may be called less stable in a variability-based sense, even if those fluctuations remain bounded.

Thus temporal variance is another stability measure, particularly in stochastic ecology.

Stability of cycles

Not every stable ecological behaviour is an equilibrium. A predator–prey model may have a stable limit cycle.

In that case, disturbances do not return the system to one fixed point. Instead, trajectories return toward the repeating orbit.

Structural stability

Structural stability asks whether the qualitative behaviour of a model changes under small changes to the equations or parameters.

A model that changes completely after a tiny parameter perturbation may be dynamically fragile even if one particular equilibrium is locally stable.

Food-web stability

For a community with many species, the Jacobian contains both self-regulation and cross-species effects. Its eigenvalues reflect the combined interaction network rather than any one pairwise interaction.

This is why food-web stability cannot be inferred simply by counting positive and negative links.

Self-regulation can stabilise systems

Negative diagonal terms in a community matrix represent self-limitation: when a species becomes more abundant, its own growth is reduced.

Strong self-regulation can help damp disturbances even when many interspecific interactions are present.

Interaction strength matters

A network with many weak interactions can behave differently from one with the same topology but several very strong interactions.

Stability therefore depends on interaction magnitudes as well as signs and network structure.

Complexity and stability

Classical ecological theory showed that sufficiently large random interaction systems can become harder to stabilise as the number and strength of interactions increase. But real food webs are not random matrices.

Modern work therefore studies how realistic network architecture, self-regulation, weak interactions and trophic structure modify this complexity–stability relationship.

Stochastic stability

Real ecosystems are continually affected by random environmental fluctuations and demographic events. A deterministic stable equilibrium can therefore become a cloud of stochastic trajectories around that equilibrium.

Stochastic stability may be studied using variance, probability of leaving a safe region, extinction probability or return-time distributions.

Critical slowing down

As some systems approach a tipping point, the dominant recovery rate can move closer to zero. Disturbances then decay more slowly.

This phenomenon is called critical slowing down and has motivated proposed early-warning indicators such as increasing recovery time, variance and autocorrelation.

Early-warning indicators are not universal guarantees. Their reliability depends on the mechanism, data quality, noise structure and type of transition.

Why stability matters biologically

Ecological management often asks more than whether an equilibrium exists. We may need to know whether a community can tolerate harvesting, habitat loss, species removal or climate variation without shifting to an undesirable state.

This requires understanding disturbance size, recovery, alternative states, extinction risk and uncertainty.

A practical analysis workflow

For a deterministic interaction model, a useful sequence is: find equilibria, determine which are biologically feasible, calculate the Jacobian, evaluate it at each equilibrium, calculate eigenvalues, interpret their real parts, then examine whether local conclusions remain meaningful under larger disturbances or stochastic variation.

Key idea. Ecological stability is not one single property. Local equilibrium stability asks whether small disturbances decay and is determined by Jacobian eigenvalues. Resistance, resilience, persistence, variability, alternative stable states and stochastic extinction describe different aspects of how ecological systems respond to disturbance.
