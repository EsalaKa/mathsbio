Host–parasite systems

A parasite lives in or on a host and obtains resources from it. The parasite benefits, while the host experiences a biological cost such as reduced growth, reproduction or survival.

Core idea. A host–parasite model connects two processes: the parasite must gain access to hosts in order to reproduce or transmit, while increasing parasite burden changes the condition, survival or reproduction of the hosts that support it.

What counts as a parasite?

Parasites include organisms with very different life histories: worms, protozoa, fungi, ectoparasites and many other organisms. Some parasites spend most of their lives within one host; others have free-living stages, vectors or several host species.

Because these life cycles differ, there is no single host–parasite equation suitable for every system.

The simplest modelling question

A useful starting point is to divide hosts into two states:

\[S(t)=\text{uninfected susceptible hosts},\qquad I(t)=\text{infected hosts}.\]

The total host population is

\[N(t)=S(t)+I(t).\]

This already resembles an epidemic model because parasitism can spread from infected to susceptible hosts.

Transmission between hosts

Under a simple mass-action assumption, new infections may occur at rate

\[\beta SI.\]

The factor \(S\) represents hosts available to become infected, \(I\) represents sources of infection, and \(\beta\) controls transmission strength.

Why the product \(SI\)? More susceptible hosts create more possible recipients, while more infected hosts create more sources of parasites. The simplest well-mixed approximation therefore makes transmission depend on both.

A basic host–parasite model

For illustration, suppose susceptible hosts are recruited at rate \(bN\), all hosts experience natural mortality \(d\), infected hosts recover at rate \(\gamma\), and parasites cause additional host mortality at rate \(\alpha\). Then

\[\boxed{\frac{dS}{dt}=bN-dS-\beta SI+\gamma I},\] \[\boxed{\frac{dI}{dt}=\beta SI-(d+\alpha+\gamma)I}.\]
SymbolMeaning
\(b\)host recruitment or birth-rate parameter
\(d\)natural host mortality rate
\(\beta\)parasite transmission coefficient
\(\gamma\)rate at which infected hosts lose infection or recover
\(\alpha\)additional host mortality caused by parasitism, often called virulence in simple models

Follow one infection event

When transmission occurs, one host moves from the susceptible class to the infected class:

\[S\longrightarrow I.\]

Therefore the same transmission term appears negatively in the susceptible equation and positively in the infected equation:

\[-\beta SI\qquad\text{and}\qquad +\beta SI.\]

The event changes infection status but does not itself create or destroy a host.

Parasite-induced mortality is different

If an infected host dies because of the parasite, that host leaves the host population. This is represented by

\[-\alpha I.\]

Thus transmission and parasite-induced mortality have fundamentally different meanings: transmission moves hosts between states, whereas mortality removes hosts.

When can infection increase?

Factor the infected-host equation:

\[\frac{dI}{dt}=I\left[\beta S-(d+\alpha+\gamma)\right].\]

When infected hosts are rare, infection increases if

\[\beta S\gt d+\alpha+\gamma.\]

It decreases if the rate of producing new infections is too small to compensate for recovery and mortality.

A threshold interpretation

The critical susceptible-host abundance in this simple density-dependent model is

\[\boxed{S_c=\frac{d+\alpha+\gamma}{\beta}}.\]

If susceptible abundance is above this threshold, a rare infection can initially increase. Below it, infection tends to decline.

Intuition. Parasite persistence is a balance between opportunities to generate new infections and the rate at which infected hosts stop contributing through recovery or death.

Connection with the basic reproduction number

Near a disease-free host population with susceptible abundance \(S_0\), the corresponding invasion quantity is

\[\mathcal{R}_0=\frac{\beta S_0}{d+\alpha+\gamma}.\]

If \(\mathcal{R}_0\gt1\), one infected host produces more than one new infected host on average while infection is rare in this simple model. If \(\mathcal{R}_0\lt1\), invasion fails deterministically.

Host–parasite feedback

Increasing host abundance can create more opportunities for parasite transmission. Increasing infection can then reduce host abundance through mortality or reduce host reproduction. Lower host abundance subsequently reduces opportunities for the parasite.

This feedback can produce equilibria, oscillations or extinction depending on the biological mechanisms and parameters.

Parasite burden: infection is not always just yes or no

For many macroparasites, an infected host may contain one worm, ten worms or hundreds of worms. A simple susceptible/infected division hides this important variation.

A model may instead track parasite burden \(W(t)\), the total number of parasites, or divide hosts according to the number of parasites they carry.

Why parasite aggregation matters

Parasites are often distributed unevenly among hosts. Many hosts may carry few or no parasites while a small fraction carry very heavy burdens.

This aggregation can strongly affect transmission, host mortality and treatment because the average parasite burden alone does not describe how parasites are distributed.

Example. An average of five parasites per host could mean every host carries about five, or that most hosts carry none while a few carry dozens. These populations can behave very differently.

Microparasites and macroparasites

Microparasite models often classify hosts by infection state because pathogens reproduce rapidly within hosts and transmission status is the main population-level variable.

Macroparasite models often need parasite counts or burden distributions because individual parasites are biologically meaningful units within the host.

Direct and indirect life cycles

A directly transmitted parasite may move from one host to another without requiring another host species. Other parasites have complex life cycles involving intermediate hosts, vectors or environmental stages.

These additional stages require extra state variables and transition terms.

Environmental transmission

Some parasites release infectious stages into the environment. Let \(E(t)\) represent environmental infectious material. A model might contain

\[\frac{dE}{dt}=\text{parasite release}-\text{environmental loss}.\]

Host infection can then depend on exposure to \(E\) rather than directly on infected hosts.

Parasite virulence

Virulence describes harm caused to the host. In a simple mortality-based model, \(\alpha\) can represent additional host mortality due to infection.

Higher virulence can sometimes increase parasite transmission if rapid within-host replication creates more infectious material, but it can also shorten the infectious period by killing the host sooner.

Transmission–virulence trade-offs

This creates an evolutionary question: a parasite may benefit from exploiting a host strongly, but excessive exploitation can remove the host on which transmission depends.

Evolutionary models can study how transmission benefits and host damage combine to favour particular parasite strategies.

Recovery and immunity

Some host–parasite interactions permit recovery. Recovered hosts may immediately become susceptible again, or they may gain temporary or long-lasting immunity.

These biological differences lead naturally to SIS-, SIR- or more complicated compartment structures.

Relation to predator–prey models

Both parasites and predators benefit from exploiting another species, and both can create feedback between consumer and resource populations.

However, parasites usually live in or on hosts, may transmit between hosts, and often do not immediately kill the host. Infection status and parasite burden therefore become important state variables that ordinary predator–prey models do not usually contain.

Relation to epidemiological models

Many infectious-disease models are host–parasite models in a broad biological sense. The same ideas of transmission, recovery, mortality, invasion thresholds and \(\mathcal{R}_0\) appear in epidemiology.

The emphasis differs: ecological host–parasite models may focus more on host population regulation, parasite burden, life cycles and coevolution.

Density-dependent versus frequency-dependent transmission

The term \(\beta SI\) represents density-dependent transmission. In some systems, the number of contacts made by an individual does not increase strongly with population density. A frequency-dependent term such as

\[\beta\frac{SI}{N}\]

may then be more appropriate.

Choosing between these forms changes the biological meaning of \(\beta\) and can change persistence thresholds.

Stochastic host–parasite models

Transmission, recovery, births and deaths occur as discrete random events. When the parasite is rare, chance can cause extinction even when the deterministic invasion condition predicts initial growth.

A continuous-time Markov chain can assign rates to infection, recovery and mortality events and calculate or simulate the resulting random trajectories.

Control and treatment

Interventions can act on different mechanisms. Treatment may increase parasite clearance, vector control may reduce transmission, sanitation may reduce environmental stages, and vaccination may reduce susceptibility or infection severity.

Mathematical models help identify which mechanism most strongly controls persistence for a particular parasite life cycle.

Assumptions and limitations

The simple model assumes homogeneous hosts, constant parameters and a particular transmission mechanism. Real systems may include age, immunity, genetic variation, spatial structure, seasonality, multiple parasite strains and highly unequal parasite burdens.

A useful host–parasite model should therefore be built around the life cycle and the biological question rather than adding complexity automatically.

Key idea. Host–parasite dynamics are driven by a balance between parasite transmission or reproduction and processes that remove infection or hosts. The simplest models resemble epidemic systems, but parasite burden, environmental stages, complex life cycles, virulence and stochastic extinction can require much richer mathematical structures.
