Lotka–Volterra equations

The Lotka–Volterra equations are one of the simplest mathematical models showing how interactions between two species can generate population cycles. The classical form describes a prey population and a predator population.

Brief historical note. Alfred J. Lotka developed related interaction equations in the 1920s while studying chemical and biological systems. Independently, the Italian mathematician Vito Volterra formulated the predator–prey equations in 1926 after being asked to explain changes in fish catches in the Adriatic Sea. Their closely related work led to the name Lotka–Volterra equations.
Core idea. Prey provide food that allows predators to increase, while predators remove prey. The two equations therefore form a feedback system.

Build the equations from the biology

Let \(N(t)\) be prey abundance and \(P(t)\) predator abundance.

If predators were absent, assume prey grow exponentially:

\[\frac{dN}{dt}=\alpha N.\]

If prey were absent, assume predators decline exponentially:

\[\frac{dP}{dt}=-\gamma P.\]

We now connect the populations through predator–prey encounters.

Why the interaction contains \(NP\)

The simplest assumption is that encounters become more frequent when either prey or predators become more numerous. The encounter rate is therefore taken to be proportional to the product

\[NP.\]

If prey double while predator abundance stays fixed, expected encounters double. If predators double while prey abundance stays fixed, expected encounters also double.

This is a mass-action assumption. It is most appropriate as a simple approximation when the populations are treated as well mixed and encounter opportunities scale with both abundances.

The prey equation

Predator encounters remove prey. If \(\beta\) measures the strength of this loss,

\[\boxed{\frac{dN}{dt}=\alpha N-\beta NP}.\]

The term \(\alpha N\) increases prey abundance, while \(\beta NP\) decreases it.

The predator equation

Predators benefit from prey consumption. If \(\delta\) measures the predator growth generated through encounters,

\[\boxed{\frac{dP}{dt}=\delta NP-\gamma P}.\]

The term \(\delta NP\) increases predators, while \(\gamma P\) represents predator mortality.

The complete system

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}\frac{dN}{dt}&=\alpha N-\beta NP,\\[3pt]\frac{dP}{dt}&=\delta NP-\gamma P.\end{aligned}}\]
SymbolMeaning
\(N\)prey population
\(P\)predator population
\(\alpha\)prey per-capita growth-rate parameter without predators
\(\beta\)strength of prey removal through predator–prey encounters
\(\gamma\)predator per-capita mortality-rate parameter without prey
\(\delta\)predator growth contribution associated with prey encounters

Factor the equations to see the thresholds

The equations can be written as

\[\frac{dN}{dt}=N(\alpha-\beta P),\] \[\frac{dP}{dt}=P(\delta N-\gamma).\]

This form immediately reveals that prey growth depends on predator abundance, whereas predator growth depends on prey abundance.

When do prey increase or decrease?

For a positive prey population, prey increase when

\[P\lt\frac{\alpha}{\beta},\]

and decrease when

\[P\gt\frac{\alpha}{\beta}.\]

Therefore \(P=\alpha/\beta\) is the predator abundance at which prey are momentarily stationary.

When do predators increase or decrease?

For a positive predator population, predators increase when

\[N\gt\frac{\gamma}{\delta},\]

and decrease when

\[N\lt\frac{\gamma}{\delta}.\]

Therefore \(N=\gamma/\delta\) is the prey abundance needed to balance predator losses.

Nullclines

The non-zero prey nullcline is

\[P=\frac{\alpha}{\beta},\]

and the non-zero predator nullcline is

\[N=\frac{\gamma}{\delta}.\]

Their intersection gives the positive coexistence equilibrium.

Equilibria

The model has two equilibria:

\[(N^*,P^*)=(0,0),\]

and

\[\boxed{(N^*,P^*)=\left(\frac{\gamma}{\delta},\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)}.\]

The second equilibrium contains both predators and prey.

Read the phase plane

The nullclines divide the plane into four regions. The direction of change in each region produces rotation around the coexistence equilibrium in the classical model.

Why the populations oscillate

Imagine prey are abundant but predators are initially scarce. Prey increase. Because prey are plentiful, predator growth then becomes positive and predators begin to increase.

Eventually predator abundance becomes large enough to make prey decline. Once prey have fallen sufficiently, predators can no longer replace their losses and predator numbers decline. Reduced predation then allows prey to recover.

Order of the cycle. Prey increase → predator food increases → predators increase → predation increases → prey decrease → predator food decreases → predators decrease → prey recover.

Predator peaks lag behind prey peaks

Numerical solution of the equations. The predator peaks occur after the prey peaks because predator growth responds to the preceding increase in prey abundance.

Why the coexistence equilibrium is unusual

In the ideal classical model, nearby trajectories do not spiral into the coexistence equilibrium and do not spiral away from it. Instead they form closed orbits.

The equilibrium is therefore neutrally stable in the classical model. Different initial conditions generally produce different closed cycles.

A conserved quantity

For positive \(N\) and \(P\), the classical system has the conserved quantity

\[\boxed{\delta N-\gamma\ln N+\beta P-\alpha\ln P=C},\]

where \(C\) remains constant along a trajectory. This mathematical property explains why the idealised trajectories form closed level curves rather than damping toward the equilibrium.

What happens if predators disappear?

Setting \(P=0\) leaves

\[\frac{dN}{dt}=\alpha N.\]

So prey grow exponentially without limit. This is one of the strongest unrealistic assumptions of the classical equations.

What happens if prey disappear?

Setting \(N=0\) leaves

\[\frac{dP}{dt}=-\gamma P,\]

so predators decline exponentially because the model provides them with no alternative food source.

A more realistic prey equation

Prey can be given logistic rather than unlimited growth:

\[\frac{dN}{dt}=rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right)-\beta NP.\]

This introduces competition among prey and a carrying capacity \(K\). It changes the equilibrium and stability properties of the system.

A more realistic predation term

The classical interaction \(\beta NP\) assumes predator consumption continues increasing linearly with prey density. Real predators spend time handling prey and can become satiated.

A saturating functional response can instead use a term such as

\[\frac{aNP}{1+ahN},\]

where \(h\) represents handling time.

Assumptions of the classical equations

The model assumes continuous populations, homogeneous mixing, constant parameters, unlimited prey growth without predators, predator dependence on this prey, and a linear mass-action encounter process.

These assumptions make the mathematics transparent but limit direct realism.

Why the model is still important

The Lotka–Volterra equations demonstrate that oscillations do not require an external periodic force. Cycles can arise internally from feedback between interacting populations.

They also provide a foundation for studying nonlinear systems, equilibria, nullclines, phase planes, stability, functional responses and more complex ecological networks.

Stochastic versions

Real predation, births and deaths occur as discrete random events. A stochastic predator–prey model can therefore produce different trajectories from the same initial populations.

Randomness becomes particularly important near low abundance, where one species may reach extinction even though the deterministic equations continue to predict positive cycles.

Key idea. The Lotka–Volterra equations are built from two simple assumptions: prey grow without predators, predators decline without prey, and encounters transfer a negative effect to prey and a positive effect to predators. Their nonlinear feedback generates the characteristic predator–prey cycle and provides a starting point for much richer ecological models.
