Exponential waiting times

In a continuous-time stochastic model, we often know the rate at which events occur but not the exact time of the next event. The exponential distribution converts that event rate into a probability distribution for the waiting time.

Core idea. If events occur at a constant rate \(a>0\) while the system remains in its current state, the waiting time \(T\) until the next event is exponentially distributed.

Start with the biological question

Suppose a stochastic epidemic is currently in a particular state. Infection and recovery are possible, but we do not know exactly when either will occur.

If the total event rate in the current state is

\[a=4\text{ events per day},\]

then events tend to occur more quickly than if

\[a=0.5\text{ events per day}.\]

But the rate alone does not specify an exact waiting time. The actual waiting time is random.

Rate and waiting time are different quantities

QuantityMeaningUnits
\(a\)event rateevents per unit time
\(T\)random time until the next eventtime

The exponential model connects them through

\[\boxed{T\sim\operatorname{Exp}(a)}.\]

The mean waiting time

For an exponential waiting time,

\[\boxed{E[T]=\frac{1}{a}}.\]

This gives an immediate interpretation of the rate.

RateMean waiting time
\(a=0.5\) per day2 days
\(a=1\) per day1 day
\(a=4\) per day0.25 day
The mean is not a schedule. If \(a=4\) per day, events do not occur exactly every 0.25 day. Individual waiting times can be shorter or much longer.

The survival probability

The most intuitive formula is often

\[\boxed{P(T>t)=e^{-at}}.\]

This is called the survival function of the waiting time. Here “survival” means that the waiting period has survived beyond time \(t\): no event has yet occurred.

Example. If \(a=2\) per day, the probability of waiting longer than one day is \(e^{-2}\approx0.135\). So there is about a 13.5% chance that no event occurs during that first day.

Probability that the event has happened by time \(t\)

The event either has happened by time \(t\), or it has not. Therefore

\[P(T\le t)=1-P(T>t).\]

Hence the cumulative distribution function is

\[\boxed{P(T\le t)=1-e^{-at}}.\]

For \(a=2\) per day and \(t=1\) day,

\[P(T\le1)=1-e^{-2}\approx0.865.\]

So the probability that an event occurs within the first day is about 86.5%.

How the rate changes the waiting-time distribution

The curves are calculated directly from \(P(T>t)=e^{-at}\). A larger rate produces a faster decline because long waits become less likely.

The graph makes the inverse relationship clear: larger rate → shorter typical waiting time.

The probability density

The exponential probability density is

\[\boxed{f_T(t)=ae^{-at}},\qquad t\ge0.\]

The density is highest near zero and decreases as \(t\) increases. This means short waiting times are more concentrated than very long waiting times.

Probability densities calculated from \(f_T(t)=ae^{-at}\). The area under each complete density curve is 1.
Density is not probability at one exact time. For a continuous random variable, \(P(T=t)=0\) for any exact value \(t\). Probabilities are calculated over intervals, such as \(P(1<T<2)\).

Probability of waiting between two times

For \(0\le s<t\),

\[P(s<T\le t)=P(T>s)-P(T>t).\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{P(s<T\le t)=e^{-as}-e^{-at}}.\]

For example, if \(a=1\) per day, the probability that the event occurs between day 1 and day 2 is

\[e^{-1}-e^{-2}\approx0.233.\]

Why does the exponential distribution arise?

Suppose the event rate is constant at \(a\). During a very short interval \(\Delta t\),

\[P(\text{event})\approx a\Delta t\]

and therefore

\[P(\text{no event})\approx1-a\Delta t.\]

To survive without an event for time \(t\), the process must repeatedly experience no event over many tiny intervals. If \(t=n\Delta t\), the approximate probability is

\[(1-a\Delta t)^n.\]

Writing \(\Delta t=t/n\), this becomes

\[\left(1-\frac{at}{n}\right)^n.\]

As \(n\to\infty\),

\[\left(1-\frac{at}{n}\right)^n\longrightarrow e^{-at}.\]

Hence

\[P(T>t)=e^{-at}.\]
This gives the intuition. A constant instantaneous event rate, applied continuously through time, produces an exponential waiting-time distribution.

The memoryless property

The exponential distribution has a remarkable property:

\[\boxed{P(T>s+t\mid T>s)=P(T>t)}.\]

Suppose we have already waited \(s\) units of time and no event has occurred. The formula says that the probability of waiting at least another \(t\) units is exactly the same as it was at the beginning.

Example. If an exponentially distributed event has not occurred during the first two hours, the remaining waiting-time distribution is not made shorter or longer simply because those two hours have already passed.

Why is it memoryless?

Using conditional probability,

\[P(T>s+t\mid T>s)=\frac{P(T>s+t)}{P(T>s)}.\]

For an exponential waiting time,

\[=\frac{e^{-a(s+t)}}{e^{-as}}=e^{-at}=P(T>t).\]

The factor involving the time already waited cancels completely.

Why memorylessness matters for CTMCs

A continuous-time Markov chain says that the present state contains all the information required to determine the future probability law.

While the CTMC remains in state \(i\), its event rates remain fixed at the values associated with that state. The exponential waiting time is memoryless, so we do not need to record how long the process has already been waiting in state \(i\).

current state→current event rates→exponential waiting time→next event

Several possible events

In a CTMC, several event types may compete. Suppose their current rates are

\[a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_m.\]

The total rate is

\[\boxed{a_0=a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_m}.\]

The waiting time until some event occurs is

\[\boxed{T\sim\operatorname{Exp}(a_0)}.\]

Once an event occurs, event \(j\) is selected with probability

\[\boxed{P(\text{event }j\text{ next})=\frac{a_j}{a_0}}.\]

SIS epidemic example

Suppose \(I(t)=i\) in a stochastic SIS model. The infection and recovery rates are

\[b(i)=\beta\frac{(N-i)i}{N},\qquad d(i)=\gamma i.\]

The total event rate is

\[a(i)=b(i)+d(i).\]

Therefore

\[T\sim\operatorname{Exp}(a(i)).\]

When the waiting time ends,

\[P(\text{infection next})=\frac{b(i)}{a(i)},\qquad P(\text{recovery next})=\frac{d(i)}{a(i)}.\]

Complete numerical SIS example

Take

\[N=100,\qquad i=10,\qquad\beta=0.30,\qquad\gamma=0.10.\]

Then

\[b(10)=2.7\text{ per day},\qquad d(10)=1.0\text{ per day},\]

so

\[a(10)=3.7\text{ per day}.\]

The next-event waiting time is

\[T\sim\operatorname{Exp}(3.7),\]

with mean

\[E[T]=\frac1{3.7}\approx0.270\text{ days}.\]

The event probabilities are

\[P(\text{infection next})=\frac{2.7}{3.7}\approx0.730,\]\[P(\text{recovery next})=\frac{1.0}{3.7}\approx0.270.\]

After that event changes the state, all rates are recalculated and a new exponential waiting time is generated.

Generating an exponential waiting time

Computer simulation starts with a uniform random number

\[U\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1).\]

An exponential waiting time with rate \(a\) can then be generated by

\[\boxed{T=-\frac{\ln U}{a}}.\]

This is called inverse-transform sampling.

Why does the simulation formula work?

The exponential cumulative distribution is

\[F(t)=1-e^{-at}.\]

Set a uniform random number equal to this cumulative probability:

\[U=1-e^{-aT}.\]

Rearranging gives

\[e^{-aT}=1-U,\]\[-aT=\ln(1-U),\]\[T=-\frac{\ln(1-U)}{a}.\]

Because \(1-U\) is also uniformly distributed on \((0,1)\), we can equivalently write

\[\boxed{T=-\frac{\ln U}{a}}.\]

A numerical simulation step

Suppose \(a=3.7\) per day and the computer generates

\[U=0.40.\]

Then

\[T=-\frac{\ln(0.40)}{3.7}\approx0.248\text{ days}.\]

The simulation clock advances by approximately 0.248 days before the next event occurs.

Do not replace the random waiting time by \(1/a\) in a stochastic simulation. The value \(1/a\) is only the mean of the exponential distribution. Using it every time would remove the randomness in event timing.

Connection to the Poisson process

If events occur according to a homogeneous Poisson process with constant rate \(\lambda\), then the times between consecutive events are independent exponential random variables:

\[T_1,T_2,T_3,\ldots\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda).\]

Thus the same random-event mechanism can be viewed in two ways:

Poisson viewExponential view
count how many events occur in time \(t\)measure how long until the next event
\(N(t)\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda t)\)\(T\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)\)
mean count \(\lambda t\)mean waiting time \(1/\lambda\)

When exponential waiting times are not appropriate

The exponential model assumes that the event hazard remains constant while we wait. It may be unsuitable when the chance of an event depends on how long the system has already been waiting.

SituationWhy exponential waiting may fail
fixed infectious periodrecovery becomes certain near a predetermined time
ageing organismdeath risk may increase with age
infection-age-dependent infectiousnessevent rate changes with time since infection
refractory periodan event may be temporarily impossible immediately after another event

Such systems may require additional state variables or different waiting-time distributions.

The full connection

event rate \(a\)→survival \(e^{-at}\)→exponential waiting time→random next-event time
Key idea. The exponential distribution translates a constant event rate into a random waiting time. Its mean is \(1/a\), its survival probability is \(e^{-at}\), and its memoryless property makes it fundamental to continuous-time Markov chains.
