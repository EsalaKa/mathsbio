Heterogeneous mixing

Heterogeneous mixing means that people do not all mix in the same way. Some individuals or groups have more contacts, different types of contacts, or stronger transmission opportunities than others.

Core idea. Homogeneous mixing treats everyone as if they interact with the population in the same average way. Heterogeneous mixing recognises that real contact patterns are uneven.

Homogeneous mixing first

In a simple SIR model, the infection term is often written

\[\beta\frac{SI}{N}.\]

This form treats the population as if susceptible and infectious individuals mix uniformly. In effect, it assumes that any susceptible person is exposed to the same average infectious environment.

Interpretation. Homogeneous mixing does not mean every person literally meets every other person. It means the model ignores systematic differences in who meets whom.

What makes mixing heterogeneous?

Mixing can differ because of age, school, household, occupation, workplace, geography, behaviour, social activity, risk status, or network position.

A simple two-group example

Suppose the population contains two groups:

GroupContact behaviour
Group Alower contact rate
Group Bhigher contact rate

If Group A averages 4 relevant contacts per day and Group B averages 12, then the two groups do not experience the same exposure opportunities.

Group B may be more likely both to acquire infection and, once infected, to transmit infection because it participates in more contacts.

Why the average can hide important structure

The average contact rate in the example is 8 contacts per day. A homogeneous model could therefore treat everyone as if they had 8 contacts.

But the real structure is different:

\[4\quad\text{contacts/day in Group A},\qquad 12\quad\text{contacts/day in Group B}.\]

Replacing these with a single average removes information about which group experiences the most transmission opportunities.

Important. Two populations can have the same overall average contact rate but very different epidemic dynamics if contacts are distributed differently across people or groups.

Heterogeneity can occur between groups

One common approach is to divide the population into groups and specify how strongly each group mixes with the others. A contact matrix can be used:

Here the diagonal values are larger, so each group mixes more strongly with itself than with the other group. This is one form of assortative mixing.

The force of infection for group \(i\) can then be written as

\[\lambda_i=q_i\sum_j c_{ij}\frac{I_j}{N_j}.\]

This allows the infection pressure experienced by one group to differ from the infection pressure experienced by another.

Heterogeneity can also occur between individuals

Not every difference needs to be represented by broad groups. Individuals can differ in the number of contacts they have. A contact network represents people as nodes and contacts as links.

The highlighted central person has many connections. In network language, this person has a high degree. If infected, such an individual may have more opportunities to pass infection onward.

Why highly connected individuals matter

Suppose one person has 2 contacts and another has 20. The second person has many more routes through which infection can enter or leave their local network.

This does not mean that every highly connected person will cause a large outbreak. Transmission is still probabilistic. But, on average, highly connected individuals can contribute disproportionately to spread.

more contacts→more exposure opportunities→more possible onward transmission

Heterogeneous susceptibility and infectiousness

Mixing is only one source of heterogeneity. Two people with the same contact pattern may still differ biologically.

Type of heterogeneityMeaning
Contact heterogeneitydifferent numbers or patterns of contacts
Susceptibility heterogeneitydifferent probability of becoming infected after exposure
Infectiousness heterogeneitydifferent probability of transmitting once infected
Outcome heterogeneitydifferent probabilities of severe disease, hospitalisation or death

A structured force of infection can separate these effects:

\[\lambda_i=s_i\sum_j c_{ij}\tau_j\frac{I_j}{N_j},\]

where \(s_i\) represents relative susceptibility of the receiving group and \(\tau_j\) represents relative infectiousness of the source group.

How heterogeneous mixing changes epidemic curves

If a highly connected group becomes infected early, transmission can initially rise faster than a homogeneous model using the same population-average contact rate might suggest. Later, that high-contact group may also lose susceptibility faster.

Heterogeneous mixing and interventions

When transmission is unevenly distributed, targeted interventions can have a different effect from uniform interventions.

For example, reducing contacts in a highly connected group may have a larger effect on transmission than applying the same contact reduction to a group with relatively few contacts.

Example. If Group B drives much of the transmission because it has a high contact rate, reducing Group B's contacts from 12 to 6 may change epidemic growth more than reducing Group A's contacts from 4 to 2, even though both are 50% reductions.

Heterogeneity and the reproduction number

In a structured population, transmission occurs through several group-to-group pathways. These can be collected into a next-generation matrix \(K\).

The basic reproduction number is then

\[\boxed{R_0=\rho(K)},\]

where \(\rho(K)\) is the spectral radius. This means that \(R_0\) summarises the combined effect of all structured transmission pathways, rather than depending on one single average contact rate.

When is heterogeneous mixing important?

It becomes especially important when contact patterns or risks differ strongly across the population, when interventions target particular groups, when age-specific or occupational effects matter, or when a small highly connected subgroup may drive much of transmission.

If mixing differences are small and the modelling question is broad, a homogeneous model may still be useful because it is simpler and requires fewer parameters.

Important limitation

Adding heterogeneity improves realism only if the additional structure is supported by data or justified assumptions. More groups and parameters can make the model harder to estimate, calibrate and interpret.

Key idea. Heterogeneous mixing means transmission opportunities are distributed unevenly. Some groups or individuals have more contact, stronger exposure, or greater transmission potential than others. Modelling that structure can change predicted epidemic growth, peak size, intervention effects and the interpretation of \(R_0\).
