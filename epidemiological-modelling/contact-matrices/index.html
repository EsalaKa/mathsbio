Contact matrices

A contact matrix records how frequently people in different population groups interact. In epidemic modelling, it is used to replace the unrealistic assumption that everybody mixes equally with everybody else.

Core idea. A contact matrix answers the question: who is mixing with whom, and how often? The epidemic model then combines those contact rates with the amount of infection present in each group.

Step 1: divide the population into groups

The groups can be defined by age, location, occupation, school year, risk level, or another characteristic relevant to transmission. Age groups are the most common example.

Suppose we use three groups:

GroupLabel
Children1
Adults2
Older adults3

Each person belongs to one group, and the population size of group \(j\) is written \(N_j\).

Step 2: define one contact rate

Let \(c_{ij}\) denote the average number of relevant contacts made by one person in group \(i\) with people in group \(j\), per unit time.

Example. If \(c_{12}=3\), then under this convention one child has, on average, three relevant contacts per day with adults.
Convention. On this page, the row index \(i\) is the group making or experiencing the contacts, and the column index \(j\) is the group contacted. This convention must always be stated because some sources use the transpose.

Step 3: place all contact rates into a matrix

We now collect all the individual contact rates into one object called the contact matrix, written \(C\).

\[C=(c_{ij}).\]

This notation means:

\(C\) is the whole matrix, while \(c_{ij}\) is one particular entry inside it. The first subscript \(i\) tells us the row, and therefore the group whose contact behaviour we are describing. The second subscript \(j\) tells us the column, and therefore the group they are contacting.

For example, \(c_{12}\) means:

row 1 = children, and column 2 = adults. Therefore \(c_{12}\) is the average contact rate of a child with adults.

Example. If \(c_{12}=3\), then the entry in row 1, column 2 is 3. This means one child has, on average, 3 relevant contacts with adults per unit time.

For three groups, the matrix contains nine contact-rate entries:

Contact with children \(j=1\)Contact with adults \(j=2\)Contact with older adults \(j=3\)
Children \(i=1\)\(c_{11}\)\(c_{12}\)\(c_{13}\)
Adults \(i=2\)\(c_{21}\)\(c_{22}\)\(c_{23}\)
Older adults \(i=3\)\(c_{31}\)\(c_{32}\)\(c_{33}\)

Each row therefore gives the complete contact pattern for one group. For example, the first row \(c_{11},c_{12},c_{13}\) describes how children contact children, adults and older adults.

The diagonal entries \(c_{11},c_{22},c_{33}\) are contacts within the same group. The off-diagonal entries describe contacts between different groups.

Step 4: interpret a numerical matrix

Consider the following illustrative matrix:

ChildrenAdultsOlder adults
Children831
Adults362
Older adults123

Read it one row at a time. The first row says that one child has, on average, 8 contacts with children, 3 with adults, and 1 with older adults. The second row gives the corresponding pattern for adults. The third row gives the pattern for older adults.

The darker diagonal cells show stronger within-group contact. This pattern is called assortative mixing.

Step 5: contact rate is not yet infection risk

A high contact rate does not automatically mean a high infection rate. We must also know how much infection is present in the contacted group.

If group \(j\) contains \(I_j\) infectious people out of \(N_j\) total people, then

\[\frac{I_j}{N_j}\]

is the infectious proportion of that group.

Therefore

\[c_{ij}\frac{I_j}{N_j}\]

is the approximate rate at which a person from group \(i\) contacts infectious people from group \(j\).

Step 6: convert infectious contacts into infection pressure

Let \(q_i\) represent the transmission/susceptibility factor for a susceptible person in group \(i\). Then one common age-specific force of infection is

\[\boxed{\lambda_i=q_i\sum_j c_{ij}\frac{I_j}{N_j}}.\]

The meaning is simple: calculate the infectious-contact contribution from every source group \(j\), add them together, then multiply by the transmission factor.

contact rate \(c_{ij}\)×infectious fraction \(I_j/N_j\)→infectious contacts×transmission factor \(q_i\)→force of infection \(\lambda_i\)

Step 7: work through one row completely

Take adults, group 2. From the illustrative matrix,

\[c_{21}=3,\qquad c_{22}=6,\qquad c_{23}=2.\]

Suppose the infectious proportions are

\[\frac{I_1}{N_1}=0.10,\qquad \frac{I_2}{N_2}=0.05,\qquad \frac{I_3}{N_3}=0.02.\]

First calculate the three infectious-contact contributions:

\[3(0.10)=0.30,\qquad 6(0.05)=0.30,\qquad 2(0.02)=0.04.\]

Add them:

\[0.30+0.30+0.04=0.64.\]

If \(q_2=0.04\), then

\[\lambda_2=0.04(0.64)=0.0256\text{ day}^{-1}.\]
Interpretation. A susceptible adult experiences an instantaneous infection rate of \(0.0256\) per day under these illustrative conditions. Over a sufficiently small interval \(\Delta t\), the infection probability is approximately \(\lambda_2\Delta t\).

Step 8: use \(\lambda_i\) in the epidemic equations

For an age-structured SIR model,

\[\frac{dS_i}{dt}=-\lambda_iS_i,\] \[\frac{dI_i}{dt}=\lambda_iS_i-\gamma_iI_i,\] \[\frac{dR_i}{dt}=\gamma_iI_i.\]

The contact matrix therefore enters the model through \(\lambda_i\). This is the mathematical bridge between observed mixing behaviour and epidemic transmission.

Why matrix entries need not look symmetric

You may expect \(c_{ij}=c_{ji}\), but per-person contact rates need not be numerically equal when the two groups have different population sizes.

For example, suppose 100 children each report 2 contacts with adults. That represents about 200 child-to-adult contact events. If there are only 50 adults, those same 200 events correspond to an average of 4 child contacts per adult.

Thus it is possible to have

\[c_{12}=2,\qquad c_{21}=4.\]

while the total number of cross-group contacts remains consistent.

Reciprocity

A commonly imposed consistency condition is

\[\boxed{N_i c_{ij}=N_j c_{ji}}.\]

The left side is the total number of contacts reported from group \(i\) to group \(j\); the right side is the total from group \(j\) to group \(i\). Real survey data may not satisfy this exactly, so matrices are often adjusted or balanced before use.

Contact matrices by setting

Contacts can be separated according to where they occur. For example,

\[C=C_{\mathrm{home}}+C_{\mathrm{school}}+C_{\mathrm{work}}+C_{\mathrm{other}}.\]

This decomposition is especially useful for modelling interventions.

SettingTypical interpretation
Homehousehold and residential contacts
Schoolcontacts generated in educational settings
Workworkplace contacts
Othertransport, leisure, retail and other social contacts

Changing a contact matrix to represent an intervention

Suppose school contacts are reduced by 60%. Instead of reducing every contact in the population, the model can modify only the school component:

\[C_{\mathrm{school,new}}=0.4C_{\mathrm{school}}.\]

The new total matrix becomes

\[C_{\mathrm{new}}=C_{\mathrm{home}}+0.4C_{\mathrm{school}}+C_{\mathrm{work}}+C_{\mathrm{other}}.\]
Why this is useful. A school intervention mainly changes contacts involving school-age groups. A uniform reduction in the whole matrix would incorrectly assume that home, work and other contacts all fall by the same amount.

How contact matrices are estimated

Contact matrices are usually estimated from data. Common sources include diary studies, contact surveys, demographic data, mobility information and setting-specific observations.

A participant may be asked how many people they contacted during a day, the age of those contacts, where the contact occurred and sometimes whether it involved physical touch or prolonged proximity. These reports are then aggregated into group-to-group average contact rates.

Important. A social contact is not automatically a transmission event. The definition of “contact” should be appropriate for the pathogen and the modelling question.

Raw contact matrix versus transmission matrix

The contact matrix describes mixing. It is not necessarily the same as the matrix that describes transmission.

A transmission-relevant matrix may also include susceptibility, infectiousness, population size, infectious duration and transmission probability. For example, an entry can be built from factors such as

\[\text{susceptibility of recipient}\times c_{ij}\times\text{infectiousness of source}.\]

This distinction is important: a group can have many contacts but low susceptibility, or few contacts but high probability of severe disease.

Contact matrices and the next-generation matrix

In a structured epidemic model, the contact matrix often contributes to a next-generation matrix \(K\). Its entry \(K_{ij}\) represents the expected number of new infections in group \(i\) caused by one infectious individual from group \(j\), under the conditions used to construct the matrix.

The overall basic reproduction number is then obtained from

\[\boxed{R_0=\rho(K)},\]

where \(\rho(K)\) is the spectral radius. The important idea is that \(R_0\) now combines all group-to-group transmission pathways.

Common mistakes

MistakeWhy it is wrong
Reading rows and columns without stating the conventionthe interpretation of \(c_{ij}\) can be reversed
Treating \(c_{ij}\) as an infection probabilityit is a contact rate; infection also depends on infectious prevalence and transmission probability
Assuming the matrix must be symmetricper-person rates can differ when group sizes differ
Changing every entry equally for a targeted interventionreal interventions often affect particular settings or groups
Using a social-contact matrix as if it were a complete transmission matrixbiological susceptibility and infectiousness may also differ between groups

The complete calculation in one chain

divide population into groups→estimate \(c_{ij}\)→form matrix \(C\)→combine with \(I_j/N_j\)→calculate \(\lambda_i\)→use \(\lambda_iS_i\) as infection flow
Key idea. A contact matrix does not itself predict infections. It describes mixing. Epidemic transmission is obtained only after those contact rates are combined with infectious prevalence and biological transmission factors. This is why contact matrices are the mathematical bridge between who meets whom and who becomes infected.
