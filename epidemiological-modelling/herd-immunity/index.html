Herd immunity

Herd immunity is a population-level reduction in transmission. It occurs when enough people are protected that an infectious person, on average, produces fewer than one new infection.

Core idea. Herd immunity does not require everyone to be immune. It requires the population to contain few enough susceptible people that infection can no longer sustain continued growth.

Start with the quantities

SymbolMeaning
\(R_0\)Average number of secondary infections produced by one infectious individual when the population is fully susceptible.
\(p\)Proportion of the population that is protected.
\(1-p\)Proportion that remains susceptible.
\(R_{\mathrm{eff}}\)Average number of secondary infections produced under the current level of susceptibility.

Why protection reduces transmission

Suppose initially \(R_0=4\). This means that, in a fully susceptible population, one infectious person produces about four new infections on average.

If 25% of the population is protected, then 75% remains susceptible. The infectious person no longer has the same opportunity to generate four new infections, because some contacts are now with protected people.

In the simple homogeneous model, this is represented by

\[\boxed{R_{\mathrm{eff}}=R_0(1-p)}.\]
Example. If \(R_0=4\) and \(p=0.25\), then \[R_{\mathrm{eff}}=4(1-0.25)=4(0.75)=3.\] So one infectious person now produces about three new infections on average rather than four.

When does herd immunity occur?

The key threshold is

\[R_{\mathrm{eff}}=1.\]

If \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}>1\), each infectious person replaces themselves with more than one new infection on average, so infection can grow. If \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}<1\), each infectious person produces fewer than one new infection on average, so transmission tends to decline.

\(R_{\mathrm{eff}}>1\): growth    |    \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}=1\): threshold    |    \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}<1\): decline

Deriving the herd-immunity threshold

Using

\[R_{\mathrm{eff}}=R_0(1-p),\]

the threshold occurs when

\[R_0(1-p)=1.\]

Solving for \(p\),

\[1-p=\frac{1}{R_0},\]\[\boxed{p_c=1-\frac{1}{R_0}}.\]

Here \(p_c\) is the critical protected proportion: the level of protection at which \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}=1\).

Example with \(R_0=4\). \[p_c=1-\frac14=0.75.\] So the threshold is 75% protected. Above 75%, the simple model gives \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}<1\).

Reading the graph

The graph below uses the same example, \(R_0=4\). Nothing new is introduced in the graph: it simply plots the equation

\[R_{\mathrm{eff}}=4(1-p).\]

The horizontal axis is \(p\), the protected proportion. The vertical axis is \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}\), the average number of new infections produced by one infectious person under that level of protection.

Read the graph from left to right:

At 0% protected, \(p=0\), so \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}=4\). At 50% protected, \(p=0.5\), so \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}=2\). At 75% protected, \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}=1\): this is the threshold. If protection rises above 75%, \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}<1\), so infection tends to decline.

Why unprotected people can benefit

An unprotected person can still become infected. The indirect benefit arises because transmission chains are more often interrupted by protected individuals, so infection has fewer routes through the population.

What the simple formula assumes

The formula \(p_c=1-1/R_0\) assumes homogeneous mixing and complete, lasting protection. Real populations may have imperfect vaccines, waning immunity, age structure, different contact patterns, or changing variants. These can alter the threshold.

If a vaccine has efficacy \(e\) against infection, a simple approximation is

\[R_{\mathrm{eff}}=R_0(1-ep),\]

giving the approximate vaccination coverage threshold

\[\boxed{p_c=\frac{1-1/R_0}{e}}.\]

The threshold does not mean zero cases

Reaching the threshold means sustained growth is no longer expected under the model assumptions. It does not mean transmission stops immediately or that no further infections can occur.

Key idea. Herd immunity is easiest to understand through \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}\): protection reduces the susceptible fraction, which reduces the average number of new infections. The threshold is reached when \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}=1\).
