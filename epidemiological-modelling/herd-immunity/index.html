Herd immunity

Herd immunity is a population-level effect. When enough people are immune or otherwise protected, an infectious person is less likely to meet someone who can be infected, so transmission becomes harder to sustain.

Important idea. Herd immunity does not mean every individual is immune. It means the susceptible fraction has become small enough that, on average, infection can no longer replace itself.

Why does immunity reduce transmission?

In the simple SIR model, the effective reproduction number is

\[R_{\mathrm{eff}}=R_0\frac{S}{N}.\]

The factor \(S/N\) is the fraction of the population still susceptible. If everyone is susceptible, \(S/N\approx1\), so \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx R_0\). As immunity increases, \(S/N\) becomes smaller, so each infectious person generates fewer new infections on average.

more immune people → fewer susceptible contacts → smaller \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}\)

Deriving the herd-immunity threshold

Suppose a fraction \(p\) of the population is completely protected. Then the susceptible fraction is

\[\frac{S}{N}=1-p.\]

Therefore

\[R_{\mathrm{eff}}=R_0(1-p).\]

For sustained epidemic growth to stop, we need

\[R_{\mathrm{eff}}<1.\]

So

\[R_0(1-p)<1.\]

Solving for \(p\) gives

\[\boxed{p>1-\frac{1}{R_0}}.\]

The critical protected proportion is therefore

\[\boxed{p_c=1-\frac{1}{R_0}}.\]
Example. If \(R_0=4\), then \(p_c=1-1/4=0.75\). In this idealised model, more than 75% of the population must be completely protected for \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}<1\).

Graph: how protection changes epidemic potential

The graph below fixes \(R_0=4\) and shows how the effective reproduction number falls as the protected proportion \(p\) increases.

The line follows

\[R_{\mathrm{eff}}=4(1-p).\]

At \(p=0\), nobody is protected and \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}=4\). As protection increases, \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}\) falls. At \(p=0.75\), \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}=1\). Beyond this point, \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}<1\), so infection tends to decline rather than grow.

Why herd immunity protects people who are not immune

An unprotected individual can still become infected. However, if many people around them are immune, chains of transmission are more likely to be interrupted before reaching them. This is the indirect protection that gives herd immunity its name.

Intuition. Herd immunity does not create a physical barrier around each susceptible person. It changes the network of possible transmission paths by making successful onward transmission less likely.

Why the simple threshold can be misleading

The formula

\[p_c=1-\frac{1}{R_0}\]

assumes homogeneous mixing, complete and permanent protection, and a fixed \(R_0\). Real populations are more complicated. Vaccine protection may be incomplete, immunity may wane, contact rates differ by age or behaviour, and pathogen variants can change transmissibility.

For an imperfect vaccine with efficacy against infection \(e\), a simple approximation is

\[R_{\mathrm{eff}}=R_0(1-ep).\]

Then the vaccination coverage needed to cross the threshold is approximately

\[\boxed{p_c=\frac{1-1/R_0}{e}},\]

provided the assumptions of that simplified model are appropriate. If this expression exceeds 1, the model indicates that vaccination with that efficacy alone cannot reduce \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}\) below 1.

Herd immunity is a threshold, not a guarantee of zero cases

Crossing the threshold means that sustained growth is not expected under the model conditions. It does not mean transmission stops instantly or that no further cases can occur. Existing infections can continue for some time, and local outbreaks can still occur in groups with lower protection.

Key idea. Herd immunity occurs when population protection reduces the effective reproduction number below 1. In the simplest homogeneous model, the threshold is \(p_c=1-1/R_0\), but the real threshold depends on how immunity, mixing and vaccine effects are represented.
