Vaccination

Vaccination is included in an epidemic model by changing the way individuals move between compartments or by changing their risk of infection, infectiousness, or disease progression. The exact mathematical form depends on what biological effect the vaccine is intended to represent.

Key modelling question. What does vaccination do in the biological system being studied: prevent infection completely, reduce susceptibility, reduce onward transmission, reduce severe disease, or provide temporary protection?

A simple vaccination model

Suppose susceptible individuals are vaccinated at per-capita rate \(\nu\). We introduce a vaccinated compartment \(V(t)\):

If \(\nu\) is the vaccination rate, then the total rate at which susceptible individuals move into \(V\) is

\[\nu S.\]

This contributes

\[-\nu S\]

to the susceptible equation and

\[+\nu S\]

to the vaccinated equation.

Complete protection

In the simplest idealised case, vaccinated individuals are fully protected from infection during the period studied. Then vaccination removes people from the susceptible pool and reduces the number available for infection.

For example, if the underlying infection term is

\[\beta\frac{SI}{N},\]

then reducing \(S\) reduces new infections directly.

Imperfect protection

Real vaccines may reduce susceptibility without eliminating it. Let \(\varepsilon\) denote the remaining susceptibility after vaccination, with

\[0\leq\varepsilon\leq1.\]

Then vaccinated individuals may still become infected at rate

\[\varepsilon\beta\frac{VI}{N}.\]

The interpretation is:

ValueMeaning
\(\varepsilon=0\)complete protection against infection
\(0<\varepsilon<1\)partial protection
\(\varepsilon=1\)no reduction in susceptibility
Example. If \(\varepsilon=0.2\), vaccinated individuals experience only 20% of the susceptibility assumed for unvaccinated susceptible individuals in this model.

Vaccination can affect more than susceptibility

A vaccine may also reduce infectiousness after breakthrough infection, reduce the probability of severe disease, shorten the infectious period, or provide protection that wanes over time. These effects require different mathematical terms.

For example, waning vaccine protection can be represented by a return flow

\[V\longrightarrow S\]

at rate \(\omega_VV\), where \(1/\omega_V\) is the mean duration of protection under the constant-rate assumption.

How vaccination changes epidemic growth

Vaccination reduces the effective susceptible population. In a simple SIR setting, if a fraction \(p\) of the population is fully protected before an outbreak, then approximately

\[\frac{S(0)}{N}=1-p.\]

The initial effective reproduction number becomes

\[\boxed{R_{\mathrm{eff}}(0)=R_0(1-p)}.\]

Therefore, sufficiently high protection can reduce the effective reproduction number below 1:

\[R_0(1-p)<1.\]

This gives the idealised critical protected fraction

\[\boxed{p_c=1-\frac{1}{R_0}}.\]
Example. If \(R_0=4\), then \(p_c=1-1/4=0.75\). In the idealised model, at least 75% of the population would need complete protection to bring the initial effective reproduction number below 1.

Why real vaccination models are more complicated

The formula above assumes complete protection, homogeneous mixing, a fixed \(R_0\), and no waning. In real populations, vaccine effectiveness can be incomplete, immunity can decline, contact patterns vary, and different outcomes such as infection, hospitalisation and death may be affected differently.

For that reason, vaccination models should be built around the biological question rather than assuming that vaccination simply moves everyone permanently into a fully immune class.

Key idea. Vaccination changes epidemic dynamics by altering susceptibility or other disease processes. The mathematical representation should match the specific biological effect being modelled, not just the fact that vaccination occurred.
