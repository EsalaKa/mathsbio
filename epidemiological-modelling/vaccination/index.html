Vaccination

Vaccination is included in an epidemic model by changing the way individuals move between compartments or by changing their risk of infection, infectiousness, or disease progression. The exact mathematical form depends on what biological effect the vaccine is intended to represent.

Key modelling question. What does vaccination do in the biological system being studied: prevent infection completely, reduce susceptibility, reduce onward transmission, reduce severe disease, or provide temporary protection?

A simple vaccination model

Suppose susceptible individuals are vaccinated at per-capita rate \(\nu\). We introduce a vaccinated compartment \(V(t)\):

If \(\nu\) is the vaccination rate, the total vaccination flow is \(\nu S\). It contributes \(-\nu S\) to the susceptible equation and \(+\nu S\) to the vaccinated equation.

Different ways to model vaccination

There is no single universal vaccination model. Several useful structures arise by asking exactly what protection vaccination provides.

1. Perfect-protection model

The simplest assumption is that vaccination gives complete protection during the modelled period:

\[S\xrightarrow{\nu S}V.\]

Vaccinated individuals no longer contribute to the susceptible pool. This is useful for understanding the basic population-level effect of vaccination, but it is often an idealisation.

2. Leaky vaccine model

A leaky vaccine reduces each vaccinated person's susceptibility but does not eliminate it. Let \(e_s\) be vaccine efficacy against infection, where \(0\le e_s\le1\). The remaining susceptibility is \(1-e_s\), so breakthrough infections occur at rate

\[\boxed{(1-e_s)\beta\frac{VI}{N}}.\]

For example, \(e_s=0.8\) means susceptibility is reduced by 80% in the model, leaving 20% of the original susceptibility.

3. All-or-nothing model

Another representation assumes that vaccination completely protects a fraction of vaccinated individuals but gives no protection to the remainder. Thus vaccine efficacy describes the fraction fully protected, rather than reducing every vaccinated person's susceptibility by the same amount.

Leaky versus all-or-nothing. With 80% efficacy, a leaky model can represent everyone receiving an 80% reduction in susceptibility, whereas an all-or-nothing model can represent 80% becoming fully protected and 20% remaining susceptible.

4. Waning-immunity model

If vaccine protection is temporary, vaccinated individuals can return to susceptibility:

\[S\xrightarrow{\nu S}V\xrightarrow{\omega_VV}S.\]

Under a constant waning rate, the mean duration of vaccine protection is \(1/\omega_V\).

5. Vaccination in an SIR or SEIR model

Vaccination can be incorporated into an existing epidemic structure. A simple SIR vaccination model may contain

\[S\rightarrow I\rightarrow R,\qquad S\rightarrow V.\]

If an exposed period matters, the infection process can instead be embedded in an SEIR-type structure:

\[S\rightarrow E\rightarrow I\rightarrow R,\qquad S\rightarrow V.\]

The vaccinated class may be fully protected or may still experience breakthrough infection, depending on the assumptions.

6. Vaccines that reduce infectiousness

A vaccine may not prevent every infection but may reduce onward transmission after breakthrough infection. If vaccinated infectious individuals are represented separately by \(I_V\), their contribution to the force of infection can be reduced, for example by a factor \(1-e_i\), where \(e_i\) is efficacy against infectiousness.

\[\lambda=\beta\frac{I_U+(1-e_i)I_V}{N}.\]

Here \(I_U\) denotes infectious unvaccinated individuals.

7. Vaccines that reduce severe disease

Sometimes the main outcome is not infection itself but hospitalisation, critical illness or death. A model can therefore allow vaccinated and unvaccinated infected individuals to have different probabilities or rates of progressing to severe disease. This is important because a vaccine can have a strong effect on severe outcomes even when protection against infection is incomplete.

How vaccination changes epidemic growth

In the simplest SIR setting, suppose a fraction \(p\) of the population is fully protected before an outbreak. Then

\[\frac{S(0)}{N}=1-p,\]

and the initial effective reproduction number becomes

\[\boxed{R_{\mathrm{eff}}(0)=R_0(1-p)}.\]

To make initial epidemic growth impossible in this idealised model, we require

\[R_0(1-p)<1,\]

which gives the critical protected fraction

\[\boxed{p_c=1-\frac{1}{R_0}}.\]
Example. If \(R_0=4\), then \(p_c=0.75\). Under the ideal assumptions, more than 75% of the population must be completely protected to make \(R_{\mathrm{eff}}(0)<1\).

Which vaccination model should be used?

The simplest model that captures the biological mechanism relevant to the question is usually preferable. If the question concerns infection prevention, susceptibility may be enough. If the question concerns hospital demand, protection against severe disease must be represented. If protection wanes, a return flow is needed. If breakthrough cases transmit differently, vaccinated and unvaccinated infectious classes may need to be distinguished.

These structures are not separate inventions belonging to a single person. They are extensions of compartmental epidemic modelling developed for different biological assumptions, so attaching one inventor to the general vaccination-model framework would be misleading.

Key idea. “Vaccination” is not one mathematical mechanism. A vaccination model should state precisely which effect is being represented and translate that effect into compartments and transition rates.
