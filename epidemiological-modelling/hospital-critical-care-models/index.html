Hospital and critical-care models

Hospital and critical-care models extend an epidemic model beyond infections. Their purpose is to translate the epidemic into questions that matter for healthcare planning: how many people may be admitted, how many beds may be occupied, when demand may peak, and whether demand may exceed available capacity.

Core idea. An epidemic curve describes infection in the population. A healthcare-demand model asks what part of that infection eventually becomes hospital and critical-care demand.

Why infection numbers alone are not enough

Not every infected person requires hospital treatment. Of those admitted to hospital, only some require critical care. Patients also remain in hospital for different lengths of time.

Therefore the number of infectious people at time \(t\) is not the same as the number of occupied hospital beds at time \(t\).

infections→severe cases→hospital admissions→critical care→discharge or death

Step 1: begin with the epidemic model

Suppose an SIR or SEIR model produces the number of new infections through time. This is the starting point for the healthcare model.

For example, in an SEIR model the infection flow is

\[\beta\frac{S(t)I(t)}{N}.\]

This gives the rate at which new infections are being generated. The healthcare model then determines what proportion of these infections later require hospital treatment.

Step 2: connect infections to hospital admissions

Let \(p_H\) be the probability that an infected person eventually requires hospital admission.

If \(J(t)\) represents new infections per day, a very simple approximation with no delay would be

\[A(t)=p_HJ(t),\]

where \(A(t)\) is the hospital admission rate.

Example. If there are 1,000 new infections and 5% eventually require hospital admission, the model expects about \(0.05\times1000=50\) hospital admissions associated with those infections.

In reality, admission usually occurs some time after infection, so a more realistic model includes an infection-to-admission delay.

Step 3: include the delay to hospital admission

Suppose the average delay from infection to hospital admission is \(d_H\) days. A simple fixed-delay approximation is

\[A(t)=p_HJ(t-d_H).\]

This shifts hospital demand later than the infection curve.

Interpretation. If infections peak today, hospital admissions need not peak today. Patients infected earlier may still be progressing towards hospitalisation.

More advanced models use a distribution of delays rather than assuming that every patient is admitted after exactly the same number of days.

Step 4: represent patients in hospital

Let \(H(t)\) be the number of patients occupying hospital beds at time \(t\). Patients enter through admissions and leave through discharge, transfer or death.

A simple hospital occupancy equation is

\[\boxed{\frac{dH}{dt}=A(t)-\delta_HH(t)}.\]
SymbolMeaning
\(H(t)\)number of occupied hospital beds
\(A(t)\)hospital admissions per unit time
\(\delta_H\)per-capita rate at which patients leave the hospital compartment
\(\delta_HH(t)\)number leaving hospital per unit time

The equation simply says

\[\text{change in hospital occupancy}=\text{admissions}-\text{departures}.\]

Why \(1/\delta_H\) represents average length of stay

If hospital departure occurs at a constant per-capita rate \(\delta_H\), the corresponding mean length of stay is

\[\boxed{L_H=\frac{1}{\delta_H}}.\]
Example. If the mean hospital stay is 10 days, then \(\delta_H=1/10=0.1\) per day. If 200 patients are currently in the hospital compartment, the modelled departure flow at that moment is \(0.1\times200=20\) patients per day.

Step 5: add critical care

Some hospital patients deteriorate and require intensive or critical care. Let \(C(t)\) denote the number of patients occupying critical-care beds.

A simple structure is:

If patients move from hospital to critical care at rate \(\alpha\), and leave critical care at rate \(\delta_C\), a simple pair of equations is

\[\frac{dH}{dt}=A(t)-\alpha H-\delta_HH,\] \[\frac{dC}{dt}=\alpha H-\delta_CC.\]

Here \(\alpha H\) is the flow from hospital to critical care.

A more detailed clinical pathway

Depending on the question, the model can distinguish general ward care, critical care, recovery and death:

infection→hospital ward→critical care→recovery or death

Some patients may recover without entering critical care, while some critical-care survivors may return to a general ward before discharge. A detailed model should include only the pathways needed for the decision being studied.

Admissions are not the same as occupancy

This distinction is essential.

QuantityMeaning
Admissionsnumber of new patients entering hospital during a period
Occupancynumber of patients already using beds at a particular time
Length of stayhow long patients remain and continue occupying beds

Even if daily admissions begin to fall, occupancy can remain high because previously admitted patients are still in hospital.

Step 6: compare demand with capacity

Let \(K_H\) be the number of hospital beds available for the modelled patient group. Capacity is exceeded whenever

\[\boxed{H(t)>K_H}.\]

Similarly, if \(K_C\) is available critical-care capacity, critical-care demand exceeds capacity whenever

\[\boxed{C(t)>K_C}.\]

The horizontal line is available capacity. The epidemic creates a changing demand curve. The period where the demand curve lies above the capacity line is the modelled period of capacity exceedance.

Peak demand

Healthcare planners are often interested in

\[H_{\max}=\max_t H(t)\]

and

\[C_{\max}=\max_t C(t).\]

These are the maximum hospital and critical-care occupancies predicted over the modelled period. The timing of the peak is also important because it indicates when the greatest pressure may occur.

How an intervention affects hospital demand

An intervention such as vaccination, reduced contact, isolation or another transmission-control measure acts earlier in the causal chain. If it reduces infections, fewer people subsequently progress to severe disease and hospitalisation.

intervention→fewer infections→fewer severe cases→fewer admissions→lower occupancy

Because of clinical delays, the effect on hospital occupancy generally appears later than the effect on infections.

Age structure matters

The probability of hospitalisation is rarely the same for every person. A structured model can use group-specific probabilities \(p_{H,i}\), for example by age:

\[A(t)=\sum_i p_{H,i}J_i(t-d_{H,i}).\]

This matters because two epidemics with the same total number of infections can produce very different hospital demand if infections occur in populations with different risks of severe disease.

Deterministic and stochastic hospital demand

In a deterministic model, the same initial conditions and parameters produce the same hospital-demand curve every time. This is useful for examining an average or expected scenario.

In a stochastic model, infection, progression and admission events can vary between simulations. Hospital peak demand therefore becomes a random quantity.

Instead of asking only

\[\text{What is the predicted peak?}\]

we can ask

\[\boxed{P\big(C_{\max}>K_C\big)},\]

the probability that peak critical-care demand exceeds capacity.

Why this matters. Two scenarios can have similar average demand but very different risks of an unusually large peak. For capacity planning, the probability of exceedance can therefore be more informative than the average trajectory alone.

Capacity is not simply the number of physical beds

A bed is useful only if the resources required to operate it are available. Depending on the application, effective capacity may also depend on staffing, equipment, oxygen, specialist facilities, infection-control requirements and beds already occupied by other patients.

For this reason, a model should define clearly what its capacity value represents.

Main assumptions and limitations

A simple hospital model may assume constant hospitalisation probabilities, constant lengths of stay, fixed capacity and identical clinical progression within each group. Real healthcare systems are more complicated. Treatment can improve over time, patient risk varies, capacity can change, and delays and lengths of stay are distributions rather than fixed values.

The model should therefore be as detailed as necessary for the question, but no more complicated than the available data can support.

The complete modelling chain

epidemic model→new infections→severity probability→clinical delay→admissions→length of stay→occupancy→capacity comparison
Key idea. Hospital and critical-care models translate transmission into healthcare pressure. The crucial distinction is that infections generate future admissions, while admissions together with length of stay generate occupancy. Capacity planning then compares that occupancy with the healthcare resources actually available.
