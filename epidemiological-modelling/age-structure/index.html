Age structure

People of different ages do not necessarily make the same number or type of contacts, have the same susceptibility to infection, or experience the same risk of severe disease. An age-structured epidemic model represents these differences by dividing the population into age groups and modelling transmission both within and between those groups.

Core idea. An ordinary compartment model asks, “How many susceptible and infectious people are there?” An age-structured model also asks, “Which age groups are they in, and which groups are interacting with which?”

Why age structure matters

A simple SIR or SEIR model commonly assumes homogeneous mixing: every individual is treated as though they have the same pattern of contact with the population. This can be a useful approximation, but it may hide important biological and social structure.

For example, school-age children may have many contacts with other children, working-age adults may mix strongly with other adults, and older adults may have fewer contacts but a greater risk of hospitalisation after infection. The same number of infections can therefore have very different consequences depending on where in the age structure those infections occur.

age→contact pattern→exposure→infection→age-specific outcome

From one population to several age groups

Suppose the population is divided into three illustrative groups: children, adults and older adults. Instead of a single susceptible population \(S(t)\), we use

\[S_1(t),\qquad S_2(t),\qquad S_3(t),\]

and similarly for infectious and recovered individuals:

\[I_1(t),I_2(t),I_3(t),\qquad R_1(t),R_2(t),R_3(t).\]

The subscript identifies the age group; it is not a power. For group \(i\),

\[N_i=S_i+I_i+R_i.\]

If the three groups contain the whole population,

\[N=N_1+N_2+N_3.\]

Compartments are repeated for each age group

Each age group has its own epidemic compartments. Infection can be generated by infectious people from the same age group or from other age groups.

The contact matrix

The central object in many age-structured transmission models is a contact matrix. Its entry \(c_{ij}\) describes the average rate of relevant contacts made by a person in age group \(i\) with people in age group \(j\).

For three age groups, a contact matrix has nine entries:

Contact with children \(j=1\)Contact with adults \(j=2\)Contact with older adults \(j=3\)
Child \(i=1\)\(c_{11}\)\(c_{12}\)\(c_{13}\)
Adult \(i=2\)\(c_{21}\)\(c_{22}\)\(c_{23}\)
Older adult \(i=3\)\(c_{31}\)\(c_{32}\)\(c_{33}\)

Rows describe the age group making or experiencing the contacts; columns describe the age group contacted. For example, \(c_{12}\) is the contact rate of children with adults under this convention.

Important. Always state the row-column convention. Different books and datasets may define matrix orientation differently.

From contacts to infection risk: the force of infection

Contact alone is not enough. A susceptible person is at risk only when contacts involve infectious people and transmission can occur. The force of infection \(\lambda_i(t)\) is the instantaneous per-person rate at which susceptible individuals in age group \(i\) acquire infection.

A common simple form is

\[\boxed{\lambda_i(t)=q_i\sum_j c_{ij}\frac{I_j(t)}{N_j}},\]

where:

QuantityMeaning
\(q_i\)transmission/susceptibility factor for a susceptible person in group \(i\)
\(c_{ij}\)contact rate between a person in group \(i\) and people in group \(j\)
\(I_j/N_j\)infectious proportion of group \(j\)
\(c_{ij}I_j/N_j\)rate of contacts from group \(i\) that are with infectious people in group \(j\)
\(\lambda_i\)total infection rate experienced by one susceptible person in group \(i\)

The summation means: calculate the contribution from every infectious age group and add those contributions together.

A numerical example of the force of infection

Consider susceptible adults, group 2. Suppose their contact rates with children, adults and older adults are respectively

\[c_{21}=3,\qquad c_{22}=6,\qquad c_{23}=2.\]

Suppose 10%, 5% and 2% of those three groups are infectious, and let \(q_2=0.04\). Then

\[\lambda_2=0.04\left[3(0.10)+6(0.05)+2(0.02)\right].\]

The three contributions inside the brackets are \(0.30\), \(0.30\) and \(0.04\). Therefore

\[\lambda_2=0.04(0.64)=0.0256\text{ day}^{-1}.\]
Interpretation. A susceptible adult has an instantaneous infection rate of \(0.0256\) per day under these illustrative conditions. Over a sufficiently short interval \(\Delta t\), the infection probability is approximately \(\lambda_2\Delta t\).

The age-structured SIR equations

Once \(\lambda_i\) is known, the ordinary SIR equations can be written separately for every age group:

\[\frac{dS_i}{dt}=-\lambda_iS_i,\] \[\frac{dI_i}{dt}=\lambda_iS_i-\gamma_iI_i,\] \[\frac{dR_i}{dt}=\gamma_iI_i.\]

The parameter \(\gamma_i\) may also depend on age if the average infectious period or removal process differs between groups. The groups are coupled through \(\lambda_i\), because \(\lambda_i\) contains infectious individuals from all groups.

Transmission and severe disease are different questions

The group that contributes most to transmission is not necessarily the group with the greatest clinical burden. To represent severe outcomes, an age-structured model can attach age-specific probabilities to infections.

If \(h_i\) is the probability that an infection in age group \(i\) requires hospital care, then a simple expected hospitalisation calculation from \(C_i\) infections is

\[H_i=h_iC_i.\]

The total is

\[H=\sum_i H_i.\]

This illustrates why age structure is particularly important when a model is used to estimate hospital demand or mortality: relatively few infections in a high-risk age group may produce substantial severe disease.

Age-specific susceptibility and infectiousness

Contact rates are only one source of heterogeneity. A more detailed force of infection may distinguish susceptibility of the recipient and infectiousness of the source:

\[\lambda_i=s_i\sum_j c_{ij}\,\tau_j\frac{I_j}{N_j},\]

where \(s_i\) represents relative susceptibility of group \(i\) and \(\tau_j\) represents relative infectiousness of infectious individuals in group \(j\). This separation prevents every age difference from being hidden inside a single transmission parameter.

Age-targeted interventions

Once age structure is included, interventions can be targeted rather than applied uniformly. For example, changing school contacts modifies entries involving children in the contact matrix; vaccinating a particular age group changes its susceptibility or disease progression; protecting high-risk older groups may reduce severe outcomes even when those groups are not the main drivers of transmission.

InterventionAge-structured representation
School closurereduce child-child and relevant child-adult contact entries
Working from homereduce selected adult-adult and adult-other contacts
Age-targeted vaccinationchange susceptibility, infectiousness or progression for selected groups
Protection of high-risk groupsreduce their exposure or probability of severe outcomes

Age structure and the reproduction number

With several interacting groups, there is no longer just one transmission pathway. New infections in one age group can generate infections in several others. These pathways can be organised in a next-generation matrix.

An entry \(K_{ij}\) represents the expected number of new infections in group \(i\) ultimately generated by one infectious individual from group \(j\), under the conditions used to construct the matrix. The basic reproduction number is obtained from the dominant eigenvalue of this matrix:

\[\boxed{R_0=\rho(K)},\]

where \(\rho(K)\) is the spectral radius, meaning the largest absolute eigenvalue. At beginner level, the important interpretation is that \(R_0\) now summarises all between-group transmission pathways together, rather than coming from one simple ratio such as \(\beta/\gamma\).

How are contact matrices obtained?

Contact matrices are usually estimated from data rather than chosen arbitrarily. Sources can include contact surveys, diaries, demographic information, mobility or setting-specific data. The definition of a “contact” must match the biological question: a conversational contact, close physical contact and a contact capable of transmitting a particular pathogen are not automatically equivalent.

Observed matrices may also require adjustment for population sizes, reciprocity, missing contacts and the settings in which contacts occur, such as home, school, work and other locations.

Choosing age groups

There is no universally correct set of age bands. Five-year bands may capture detailed variation but require more data and parameters. Broad groups are easier to interpret but can hide important differences. The choice should be driven by the disease, available data and the decision the model is intended to support.

Modelling trade-off. More age groups give more detail, but they also create more compartments, more contact-matrix entries and more parameters to estimate.

From a simple model to an age-structured model

The conceptual progression is:

one SIR/SEIR population→split into age groups→repeat compartments→add contact matrix→calculate age-specific infection rates→add age-specific outcomes or interventions

Age structure therefore does more than divide a graph by age. It changes the transmission mechanism itself by specifying who mixes with whom.

Key idea. Age-structured epidemic models combine age-specific compartments with a contact matrix. The contact matrix determines how infectious people in one group contribute to infection risk in another, while age-specific biological and clinical parameters can represent differences in susceptibility, recovery and severe outcomes. This allows the model to study both who drives transmission and who experiences the greatest burden.
