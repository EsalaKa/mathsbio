Tumour growth

Mathematical tumour-growth models describe how tumour cell number, mass or volume changes through time. Their purpose is not to give one universal law for cancer, but to provide simplified growth rules that can be compared with data and biological mechanisms.

Core intuition. The main modelling question is: how does the growth rate change as the tumour becomes larger? Different growth laws make different assumptions about this dependence.

Choose the state variable first

Let \(N(t)\) denote a measure of tumour size. Depending on the study, \(N\) might represent cell number, volume, mass or another measurable quantity.

The basic form is

\[\boxed{\frac{dN}{dt}=\text{net tumour growth rate}.}\]

The right-hand side combines proliferation and loss into one net growth law.

Exponential growth

The simplest model assumes that every tumour cell contributes the same constant net growth rate:

\[\boxed{\frac{dN}{dt}=rN.}\]

Its solution is

\[\boxed{N(t)=N_0e^{rt}.}\]

Here \(r>0\) is the net per-capita growth rate.

Why exponential growth accelerates

If the tumour doubles in size, there are twice as many cells contributing to future growth. Therefore the absolute growth rate

\[rN\]

also doubles.

This creates accelerating growth and a constant doubling time

\[\boxed{t_d=\frac{\ln2}{r}.}\]

Where exponential growth is useful

It can be a useful approximation over an early interval when resource limitation and spatial constraints are not yet strongly affecting growth.

However, indefinite exponential growth is biologically unrealistic because it predicts unlimited acceleration.

Logistic growth

The logistic model introduces a carrying-capacity scale \(K\):

\[\boxed{\frac{dN}{dt}=rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right).}\]

When \(N\ll K\), the factor \(1-N/K\) is close to 1, so growth is approximately exponential.

As \(N\) approaches \(K\), the factor tends to zero and net growth slows.

Logistic solution

For initial size \(N_0\),

\[\boxed{N(t)=\frac{K}{1+\left(\frac{K-N_0}{N_0}\right)e^{-rt}}.}\]

The curve is sigmoidal: slow at first if the initial tumour is very small, then faster, then slower again as it approaches \(K\).

Where logistic growth is fastest

The logistic growth rate as a function of tumour size is

\[g(N)=rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right).\]

Differentiate with respect to \(N\):

\[g'(N)=r\left(1-\frac{2N}{K}\right).\]

Setting \(g'(N)=0\) gives

\[\boxed{N=\frac{K}{2}.}\]

So the absolute logistic growth rate is largest when tumour size is half of the carrying-capacity scale.

Gompertz growth

The Gompertz model is

\[\boxed{\frac{dN}{dt}=rN\ln\left(\frac{K}{N}\right).}\]

Its solution can be written

\[\boxed{N(t)=K\exp\left[\ln\left(\frac{N_0}{K}\right)e^{-rt}\right].}\]

Like the logistic model, it approaches a limiting scale \(K\), but the slowing pattern is different.

What makes Gompertz different?

The Gompertz per-capita growth rate is

\[\frac1N\frac{dN}{dt}=r\ln\left(\frac{K}{N}\right).\]

This decreases continuously as \(N\) increases.

Thus the model assumes that growth becomes progressively less favourable as tumour size increases.

How to read this comparison

The exponential model keeps accelerating because it has no limiting scale.

The logistic and Gompertz models both slow as tumour size increases, but they do so differently. Therefore similar early data can lead to different long-term extrapolations.

Important modelling point. A model that fits observed data over one time interval may still give poor predictions outside that interval. Extrapolation should be treated cautiously.

Absolute growth versus per-capita growth

It is useful to distinguish

\[\frac{dN}{dt}\]

from

\[\frac1N\frac{dN}{dt}.\]

The first is total growth per unit time. The second is growth per unit tumour size.

Per-capita growth reveals the assumptions

These curves are generated from the exact per-capita growth functions. Exponential growth assumes a constant rate per unit tumour size, while logistic and Gompertz growth assume decreasing per-capita growth as the tumour enlarges.

Why growth may slow biologically

Several mechanisms can contribute to slower net growth at larger tumour size, including nutrient limitation, oxygen limitation, spatial crowding, necrosis, immune interactions, mechanical constraints and changes in the fraction of proliferating cells.

A simple growth law compresses many such mechanisms into a small number of parameters.

Carrying capacity is a model parameter

In logistic and Gompertz models, \(K\) is a limiting scale in the equation. It should not automatically be interpreted as a fixed physical maximum imposed by one specific biological mechanism.

Its interpretation depends on the data, model and experimental setting.

Volume and radius

If a tumour is approximated as a sphere of radius \(R\), then volume is

\[V=\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3.\]

Therefore a growth law written for tumour radius is not mathematically identical to the same-looking law written for volume.

The state variable must be defined clearly before parameters are interpreted.

Treatment as an additional loss term

A simple mathematical extension adds a treatment-related loss term \(T(N,t)\):

\[\boxed{\frac{dN}{dt}=G(N)-T(N,t).}\]

Here \(G(N)\) is the untreated growth law.

For example, a simple proportional loss term could be written

\[T(N,t)=u(t)N,\]

where \(u(t)\) is a modelled time-dependent treatment effect.

This is a modelling structure rather than a clinical dosing rule.

Why treatment response may not be immediate

A measured tumour volume may continue changing after a biological intervention because cells can die with delays, damaged cells may remain physically present, and measurement reflects aggregate tissue rather than instant viable-cell number.

More mechanistic models may therefore separate proliferating, damaged, dying and necrotic populations.

Two-population tumour models

A simple extension distinguishes sensitive and resistant populations:

\[S(t),\qquad R(t).\]

Each population can have its own growth and treatment-response parameters, and transitions or competition can be added when biologically justified.

Such models help study heterogeneity rather than assuming every tumour cell behaves identically.

Spatial tumour models

Ordinary differential equations treat the tumour as well mixed. Spatial models can represent tumour density \(n(x,t)\), nutrient concentration, diffusion, invasion and interactions with surrounding tissue.

A generic reaction–diffusion form is

\[\frac{\partial n}{\partial t}=D\nabla^2n+f(n).\]

This can model local proliferation together with spatial spread.

Stochastic tumour models

When populations are small or rare mutations matter, random birth, death and mutation events may be important.

Stochastic models can describe probabilities of extinction, establishment, resistant-clone emergence or variation between repeated realisations.

Parameter estimation

Growth parameters are commonly estimated by fitting model predictions to longitudinal tumour measurements.

Different models can fit the same data similarly well, especially when few observations are available. Model comparison and uncertainty analysis are therefore important.

Measurement error

Tumour size measurements are imperfect. Imaging resolution, segmentation, irregular geometry and biological variability all introduce uncertainty.

A realistic modelling analysis therefore combines the growth equation with a statistical observation model.

Identifiability

A parameter is identifiable when the available data contain enough information to estimate it reliably.

For example, if measurements cover only the early growth phase, the limiting scale \(K\) may be poorly determined because the data have not yet shown clear slowing.

Model selection matters

Exponential, logistic and Gompertz models can all describe growth over some ranges, but they encode different assumptions. The best choice should depend on biological context, data quality, time span and the purpose of the model.

A practical modelling workflow

Define what tumour size variable is being measured. Plot the longitudinal data. Start with a simple growth law. Fit parameters and inspect residuals. Compare alternative models rather than assuming one form is correct. Quantify uncertainty. Add treatment, heterogeneity, spatial structure or stochasticity only when the biological question and data support the extra complexity.

Key idea. Tumour-growth models differ mainly in how they assume growth slows as tumour size changes. Exponential growth has constant per-capita growth, logistic growth decreases linearly with size, and Gompertz growth decreases logarithmically. The appropriate model is an empirical and biological question, not a universal choice.
