Scaling and nondimensionalisation

Biological models use quantities with units: population may be measured in individuals, time in days, and rates per day. These quantities are essential for describing the real system. However, when analysing a model, we often want to see its relative behaviour rather than being distracted by a particular population size, time unit or numerical scale.

The central idea. Scaling asks, “How large is this quantity compared with a natural reference value?” Nondimensionalisation uses these scaled quantities to rewrite the model without physical units.

Why do we scale?

Suppose a population has carrying capacity \(K=1000\) individuals. A population of \(N=500\) can be described as 500 individuals, but we can also say that it is half of its carrying capacity:

\[ x=\frac{N}{K}=\frac{500}{1000}=0.5. \]

The second description tells us something immediately about the population's position relative to its natural scale.

Thus we choose \(K\) as a population scale and define

\[ x=\frac{N}{K}. \]

Now \(x=0.2\) means 20% of carrying capacity, \(x=0.5\) means 50%, and \(x=1\) means the population is at carrying capacity.

Because both \(N\) and \(K\) have units of individuals, their units cancel. Therefore \(x\) is dimensionless.

Scaling time

If \(r\) is a growth rate with units time\(^{-1}\), then \(1/r\) has units of time and provides a natural time scale. We define

\[ \tau=rt=\frac{t}{1/r}. \]

Thus \(\tau\) measures time relative to the natural growth time \(1/r\). For example, \(\tau=1\) means one growth time scale has passed, and \(\tau=2\) means two have passed.

The units also cancel:

\[ [\tau]=\text{time}^{-1}\times\text{time}=1. \]

How scaling produces nondimensionalisation

A dimensional quantity becomes dimensionless when it is compared with a suitable scale having the same units:

quantity ÷ natural scale → scaled dimensionless quantity
\[ x=\frac{N}{K},\qquad \tau=\frac{t}{1/r}=rt. \]

This is why scaling is needed in the nondimensionalisation process. We first choose meaningful scales; dividing by those scales removes the units.

Example: logistic growth

Consider

\[ \frac{dN}{dt}=rN\left(1-\frac{N}{K}\right). \]

Choose the natural population and time scales

\[ K,\qquad \frac{1}{r}, \]

and define

\[ x=\frac{N}{K},\qquad \tau=rt. \]

Equivalently, \(N=Kx\). Since \(d\tau/dt=r\),

\[ \frac{dN}{dt}=Kr\frac{dx}{d\tau}. \]

Substituting into the logistic equation gives

\[ Kr\frac{dx}{d\tau}=Kr\,x(1-x), \]

so

\[ \boxed{\frac{dx}{d\tau}=x(1-x)}. \]

How to interpret the dimensionless equation

The equation

\[ \frac{dx}{d\tau}=x(1-x) \]

has a direct interpretation because both variables are relative quantities:

\(x\) tells us the fraction of carrying capacity, while \(\tau\) tells us time measured in natural growth-time units.

Example. If \(x=0.5\), the population is at half its carrying capacity. At this point, \[ \frac{dx}{d\tau}=0.5(1-0.5)=0.25. \] The value \(0.25\) is a dimensionless rate of change: relative population size is increasing at 0.25 per unit of scaled time.

The structure is now easy to read. When \(x\) is close to 0, the population can grow. As \(x\) approaches 1, the factor \(1-x\) approaches 0, so growth slows. At \(x=1\),

\[ \frac{dx}{d\tau}=0, \]

which means the population has reached carrying capacity.

Why is this useful?

The original model contains the particular values \(r\) and \(K\). The dimensionless model focuses on the shape and relative behaviour of logistic growth. Two populations can have very different carrying capacities and growth rates but still obey the same scaled equation.

Nondimensionalisation can therefore make models easier to interpret and compare, reduce the number of parameters appearing explicitly, and reveal which parameter combinations actually control the behaviour.

The parameters have not disappeared biologically. Their roles have been absorbed into the scales: \(K\) determines what counts as one unit of population and \(1/r\) determines what counts as one unit of time.

Returning to real biological quantities

After analysing the dimensionless model, we can recover the original quantities:

\[ N=Kx,\qquad t=\frac{\tau}{r}. \]

For example, if \(K=1000\) and the dimensionless result gives \(x=0.8\), then

\[ N=1000(0.8)=800\text{ individuals}. \]
In one sentence. We scale to express biological quantities relative to natural reference values; this removes units and produces a dimensionless model whose underlying behaviour is often easier to see, interpret and compare.
