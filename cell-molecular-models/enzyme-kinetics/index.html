Enzyme kinetics

Enzyme kinetics is the mathematics of how quickly enzyme-catalysed reactions occur. The central question is simple: if we change the amount of substrate, how does the reaction rate change?

Core intuition. Think of enzymes as a limited number of molecular working sites. At low substrate concentration many enzymes are waiting for substrate. At high substrate concentration almost every enzyme is already occupied, so adding more substrate has progressively less effect.

What an enzyme does

Suppose a substrate molecule \(S\) is converted into a product \(P\). Without an enzyme the reaction may be slow. An enzyme \(E\) binds the substrate, forms an enzyme–substrate complex \(ES\), and helps the conversion occur.

\[E+S\;\underset{k_{-1}}{\overset{k_1}{\rightleftharpoons}}\;ES\;\overset{k_2}{\longrightarrow}\;E+P\]

The enzyme appears again after product formation. It is therefore a catalyst rather than something permanently consumed by the reaction.

Read the reaction one step at a time

StepMeaningRate
\(E+S\to ES\)enzyme and substrate bind\(k_1[E][S]\)
\(ES\to E+S\)complex separates without product\(k_{-1}[ES]\)
\(ES\to E+P\)complex forms product\(k_2[ES]\)

The square brackets mean concentration. For example, \([S]\) is substrate concentration.

Why is the binding rate \(k_1[E][S]\)?

Binding requires an enzyme molecule and a substrate molecule to encounter one another. If there is more enzyme, encounters become more frequent. If there is more substrate, they also become more frequent. Under mass-action kinetics this gives

\[\text{binding rate}=k_1[E][S].\]

This is the same basic idea used throughout biochemical reaction-network modelling.

Write the differential equations

Each reaction either adds to or removes from a molecular species. For the complex,

\[\boxed{\frac{d[ES]}{dt}=k_1[E][S]-(k_{-1}+k_2)[ES].}\]

The first term creates complexes. The other two processes remove them.

Product is created only by the final reaction:

\[\boxed{\frac{d[P]}{dt}=k_2[ES].}\]

Therefore the instantaneous reaction velocity is

\[\boxed{v=\frac{d[P]}{dt}=k_2[ES].}\]

The enzyme is conserved

An enzyme molecule is either free or temporarily bound in a complex. Therefore

\[[E]_T=[E]+[ES],\]

where \([E]_T\) is the total enzyme concentration. Hence

\[[E]=[E]_T-[ES].\]

This conservation law is important because the number of available enzyme molecules is limited.

What happens immediately after substrate is added?

Initially there may be almost no \(ES\). Enzyme and substrate begin binding, so \([ES]\) rises. After a short transient, complex formation and complex removal can become approximately balanced.

This motivates the quasi-steady-state approximation

\[\frac{d[ES]}{dt}\approx0.\]

Deriving the rate law

Using the complex equation,

\[0\approx k_1[E][S]-(k_{-1}+k_2)[ES].\]

Define

\[K_m=\frac{k_{-1}+k_2}{k_1}.\]

Then

\[[E][S]\approx K_m[ES].\]

Substitute \([E]=[E]_T-[ES]\):

\[([E]_T-[ES])[S]=K_m[ES].\]

Rearranging gives

\[\boxed{[ES]=\frac{[E]_T[S]}{K_m+[S]}.}\]

Since \(v=k_2[ES]\), define

\[V_{\max}=k_2[E]_T.\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{v=\frac{V_{\max}[S]}{K_m+[S]}.}\]

Understanding the curve before memorising it

At very low substrate concentration, most enzymes are unoccupied. Adding substrate strongly increases the chance of binding, so the reaction rate rises almost proportionally to \([S]\).

At high substrate concentration, most enzymes are occupied most of the time. The enzyme supply becomes the bottleneck. The reaction rate approaches a ceiling, \(V_{\max}\).

The graph is generated directly from \(v=V_{\max}[S]/(K_m+[S])\), using \(V_{\max}=1\) and \(K_m=2\). The marked point is calculated from the same function.

What exactly is \(V_{\max}\)?

When substrate is abundant, nearly all enzyme is tied up as \(ES\). Thus \([ES]\) approaches \([E]_T\), and

\[v=k_2[ES]\to k_2[E]_T=V_{\max}.\]

So \(V_{\max}\) is controlled by how much enzyme is present and how rapidly bound enzyme produces product.

What exactly is \(K_m\)?

Set \([S]=K_m\) in the Michaelis–Menten equation:

\[v=\frac{V_{\max}K_m}{K_m+K_m}=\frac{V_{\max}}2.\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{[S]=K_m\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v=\frac{V_{\max}}2.}\]

This gives \(K_m\) a very useful graphical interpretation: it is the substrate concentration at half the maximum velocity.

Careful. \(K_m\) is often discussed informally as an indication of enzyme–substrate affinity, but it is defined by \((k_{-1}+k_2)/k_1\). It is not universally identical to a simple binding dissociation constant.

Low-substrate approximation

If \([S]\ll K_m\), then \(K_m+[S]\approx K_m\), so

\[v\approx\frac{V_{\max}}{K_m}[S].\]

The rate is approximately linear in substrate concentration.

High-substrate approximation

If \([S]\gg K_m\), then \(K_m+[S]\approx[S]\), giving

\[v\approx V_{\max}.\]

Adding still more substrate now produces very little increase in rate. This is enzyme saturation.

Why doubling substrate does not always double the rate

At low \([S]\), doubling substrate can approximately double the rate. Near saturation, however, most enzyme molecules are already occupied. Doubling substrate cannot double the number of enzymes, so the reaction rate changes much less.

What happens if we add more enzyme?

Because

\[V_{\max}=k_2[E]_T,\]

doubling total enzyme concentration doubles \(V_{\max}\), provided the other assumptions remain appropriate. It does not automatically change \(K_m\).

Initial-rate experiments

Michaelis–Menten kinetics is commonly studied using the initial reaction rate. Experiments are repeated with different starting substrate concentrations, and the early rate of product formation is measured before substrate depletion and product accumulation become important.

The resulting pairs \(([S],v)\) can be used to estimate \(V_{\max}\) and \(K_m\).

Units

Reaction velocity has units of concentration per unit time. \(K_m\) has units of concentration. \(V_{\max}\) has the same units as reaction velocity.

The rate constants have units determined by their reaction order: \(k_{-1}\) and \(k_2\) have units of inverse time, while the bimolecular binding constant \(k_1\) has units of inverse concentration per unit time.

What assumptions are being made?

The standard derivation assumes an initial-rate regime, mass-action binding, approximately constant total enzyme, and a quasi-steady concentration of enzyme–substrate complex after the initial transient. The simplest form also ignores complications such as cooperativity, multiple substrates, strong product inhibition and allosteric regulation.

When Michaelis–Menten is not enough

Real enzymes can have several binding sites, cooperate between sites, be inhibited, switch conformations, interact with several substrates or operate inside complex biochemical networks. These mechanisms lead to richer kinetic models.

The value of Michaelis–Menten kinetics is that it gives a remarkably clear first model linking a molecular mechanism to an experimentally measurable nonlinear rate law.

Key idea. The curve saturates because enzyme molecules are limited. At low substrate, substrate availability limits the reaction. At high substrate, enzyme capacity limits it. \(K_m\) tells us where the rate reaches half of \(V_{\max}\), and \(V_{\max}\) is the limiting rate when enzyme is saturated.
