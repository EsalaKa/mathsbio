Natural selection

Natural selection changes the frequencies of heritable types when those types differ in reproductive success. Mathematics makes this idea precise by tracking how frequencies change from one generation to the next.

Core intuition. A type does not increase simply because it reproduces. It increases in frequency when its reproductive success is greater than the population average.

Start with two types

Suppose a population contains two heritable types, \(A\) and \(a\). Let

\[p=\text{frequency of }A,\qquad q=\text{frequency of }a.\]

Because there are only two types,

\[\boxed{p+q=1,\qquad q=1-p.}\]

What is fitness?

Let \(w_A\) and \(w_a\) be the fitnesses of the two types. In this simple model, fitness represents expected reproductive contribution to the next generation.

If type \(A\) has frequency \(p\), its contribution after selection is proportional to

\[pw_A.\]

The contribution of type \(a\) is proportional to

\[(1-p)w_a.\]

Why we must divide by mean fitness

The quantities \(pw_A\) and \((1-p)w_a\) are not yet frequencies because they do not generally sum to 1.

Their sum is the population mean fitness:

\[\boxed{\bar w=pw_A+(1-p)w_a.}\]

Therefore the next-generation frequency of \(A\) is

\[\boxed{p'=\frac{pw_A}{\bar w}.}\]

Similarly,

\[(1-p)'=\frac{(1-p)w_a}{\bar w}.\]

The general selection equation

For many types with frequencies \(p_i\) and fitnesses \(w_i\),

\[\boxed{p_i'=\frac{p_iw_i}{\bar w},\qquad \bar w=\sum_jp_jw_j.}\]

Normalisation by \(\bar w\) ensures that all next-generation frequencies still sum to 1.

When does a type increase?

Compare \(p'\) with \(p\):

\[p'-p=p\left(\frac{w_A}{\bar w}-1\right).\]

Therefore, for \(p>0\),

\[\boxed{w_A>\bar w\Rightarrow p'>p.}\]

A type increases in frequency precisely when its fitness is above the current population mean.

Selection coefficient

A common two-type model sets

\[w_A=1+s,\qquad w_a=1,\]

where \(s\) is the selection coefficient.

If \(s>0\), type \(A\) is favoured. Then

\[\bar w=p(1+s)+(1-p)=1+sp,\]

and

\[\boxed{p'= rac{p(1+s)}{1+sp}.}\]

Change in one generation

Subtract \(p\):

\[\Delta p=p'-p.\]

Using the previous expression,

\[\boxed{\Delta p=\frac{sp(1-p)}{1+sp}.}\]

This formula contains several important ideas at once.

Why selection is weak near p = 0 or p = 1

The factor

\[p(1-p)\]

is small when the favoured type is extremely rare or already nearly fixed.

Selection changes frequency fastest at intermediate frequencies, although the exact maximum depends slightly on the denominator \(1+sp\).

Allele frequency across generations

Starting from \(p_0\), the recurrence

\[p_{n+1}=\frac{p_n(1+s)}{1+sp_n}\]

generates the allele frequency generation by generation.

Each curve is generated by iterating the stated selection recurrence from the same initial frequency. Stronger positive selection increases the allele frequency more rapidly.

An exact formula using odds

Define the odds of allele \(A\) as

\[O_n=\frac{p_n}{1-p_n}.\]

Under constant relative fitness \(1+s\),

\[\boxed{O_{n+1}=(1+s)O_n.}\]

Therefore

\[O_n=O_0(1+s)^n.\]

Converting back to frequency gives

\[\boxed{p_n=\frac{p_0(1+s)^n}{1-p_0+p_0(1+s)^n}.}\]

This exact formula explains the S-shaped rise of a favoured allele when frequency is plotted against generation.

Why fixation takes time

In the deterministic recurrence, a beneficial allele approaches frequency 1 gradually. For finite \(n\), it generally remains below 1 unless it started at 1.

Selection can therefore make an allele very common without making it mathematically equal to 1 in a finite number of generations.

Selection is relative

Suppose every type's fitness is multiplied by the same positive constant \(c\). Then

\[p_i'=\frac{p_i(cw_i)}{\sum_jp_j(cw_j)}=\frac{p_iw_i}{\bar w}.\]

So only relative fitness differences matter for frequency change in this model.

Frequency-dependent selection

Fitness need not be constant. It can depend on the current population composition:

\[w_A=w_A(p),\qquad w_a=w_a(p).\]

Then a type can be favoured when rare but disadvantaged when common, or vice versa.

This can create internal equilibria where both types persist.

An equilibrium condition

An allele-frequency equilibrium satisfies

\[p'=p.\]

For an internal equilibrium with \(0\[\boxed{w_A(p)=\bar w.}\]

For a two-type system, that is equivalent to

\[w_A(p)=w_a(p).\]

Diploid selection

For a diploid locus with alleles \(A\) and \(a\), the genotypes are

\[AA,\qquad Aa,\qquad aa.\]

If allele \(A\) has frequency \(p\) and random mating produces Hardy–Weinberg genotype frequencies, then before selection

\[AA:p^2,\qquad Aa:2p(1-p),\qquad aa:(1-p)^2.\]

Genotype fitnesses

Let genotype fitnesses be

\[w_{AA},\qquad w_{Aa},\qquad w_{aa}.\]

After selection, their contributions are proportional to

\[p^2w_{AA},\qquad2p(1-p)w_{Aa},\qquad(1-p)^2w_{aa}.\]

The mean fitness is

\[\boxed{\bar w=p^2w_{AA}+2p(1-p)w_{Aa}+(1-p)^2w_{aa}.}\]

Allele frequency after diploid selection

The frequency of allele \(A\) among the selected population is

\[\boxed{p'=\frac{p^2w_{AA}+p(1-p)w_{Aa}}{\bar w}.}\]

This equation allows dominance, recessivity, heterozygote advantage and heterozygote disadvantage to be modelled.

Dominance

A common parameterisation is

\[w_{AA}=1+s,\qquad w_{Aa}=1+hs,\qquad w_{aa}=1.\]

The dominance coefficient \(h\) determines how much of the selective advantage appears in the heterozygote.

For example, \(h=1\) makes the beneficial effect dominant in this parameterisation, while \(h=0\) makes it recessive.

Why a recessive beneficial allele can rise slowly when rare

If a beneficial allele is recessive, most copies are hidden in heterozygotes when the allele is rare. Selection then has little effect on those copies.

This is a mathematical reason why dominance can strongly affect the speed of allele-frequency change.

Heterozygote advantage

If

\[w_{Aa}>w_{AA},\qquad w_{Aa}>w_{aa},\]

selection can maintain both alleles at a stable internal equilibrium.

This is called balancing selection because selection itself can preserve genetic variation.

Heterozygote disadvantage

If the heterozygote has lower fitness than both homozygotes, an internal equilibrium may exist but be unstable.

The long-term outcome can then depend strongly on the initial allele frequency.

Mean fitness and selection

In simple constant-fitness selection models, selection tends to increase the contribution of higher-fitness types. However, statements about mean fitness always depend on model assumptions.

When fitness is frequency-dependent, environments change, or mutation and migration occur, the dynamics can be more complicated.

Selection versus genetic drift

The equations above are deterministic: once the starting frequency and parameters are fixed, the next frequency is fixed.

In finite populations, reproduction is random. Genetic drift can increase or decrease allele frequency by chance.

A beneficial allele can therefore be lost when rare even though deterministic selection favours it.

Selection versus mutation

Mutation creates new alleles or converts one type into another. Selection changes frequencies because reproductive success differs.

When both operate, an allele can be maintained at mutation–selection balance rather than simply fixing or disappearing.

Selection versus migration

Migration moves alleles between populations. Selection may favour an allele locally while migration continually introduces another allele.

The resulting equilibrium reflects both processes.

Continuous-time approximation

For weak selection, a common approximation is

\[\boxed{\frac{dp}{dt}=sp(1-p).}\]

This has the logistic-form solution

\[\boxed{p(t)=\frac{p_0e^{st}}{1-p_0+p_0e^{st}}.}\]

It is a continuous-time approximation to directional selection, not the same model as the discrete-generation recurrence.

Discrete and continuous selection compared

The discrete points are generated from the exact recurrence, while the smooth curve is generated from the continuous approximation using the same starting frequency and selection parameter.

Fitness is context-dependent

Fitness is not an absolute property of a genotype. It depends on the environment, competing types, life-history stage and the particular reproductive outcome represented by the model.

A type can therefore be favoured in one environment and disfavoured in another.

What natural-selection models can study

They can investigate adaptation, fixation, maintenance of polymorphism, dominance, frequency dependence, resistance evolution, life-history trade-offs and interactions between selection, mutation, migration and drift.

A practical modelling workflow

Define the heritable types and their frequencies. Specify the biological meaning of fitness. Calculate the mean fitness. Normalise reproductive contributions to obtain next-generation frequencies. Find equilibria and determine which types increase when rare. Add dominance, frequency dependence, mutation, migration or drift only when required by the biological question.

Key idea. Natural selection is a change in relative frequency caused by differences in reproductive success. The central equation \(p_i'=p_iw_i/\bar w\) says that a type grows in frequency when its fitness is greater than the current population mean. Selection acts on differences, while the population composition determines how those differences translate into evolutionary change.
